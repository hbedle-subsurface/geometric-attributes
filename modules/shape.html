Module 06  ·  Geometric attributes

Curvedness and shape index: how much, and what shape

Module 05 left you with two numbers at every sample, k₁ and k₂, and two numbers are hard to look at. Recombining them into how strongly it bends and what shape it bends into turns out to be far easier to interpret — and the shape half of that pair asks a question no single curvature volume can answer: is this a dome, a ridge, a saddle, a valley or a bowl?

1The same information, asked differently

From k₁ and k₂ to magnitude and shape

Every point on a reflector carries two principal curvatures. Plot them as a point on a graph with k₁ across and k₂ up, and the whole vocabulary of shapes falls out of where the point sits rather than what its coordinates are.

Two things about that point turn out to matter, and they are independent of one another. How far it is from the origin says how strongly the surface is bent — that is curvedness. Which direction it lies in says what shape it is bent into — that is the shape index. A dome and a bowl sit on opposite sides; a saddle sits on the diagonal where k₁ and k₂ are equal and opposite; and the origin is a flat plane, where the shape question has no answer at all.

2The shape scale, end to end

Five names on one number line

Shape index runs from −1 to +1 and every value along it has a name. Walk the preset buttons in the header from Dome to Bowl and watch the marker travel down the scale while the little surface in the header morphs from one shape into the next.

The useful thing about this scale is that it is continuous. Real geology is not obligingly one of five shapes; it sits somewhere between them, and the number tells you where. A value of +0.7 is a dome that has been stretched a bit, +0.3 is a ridge with a slight bowl to it, and so on. The five names are landmarks on a continuum, not boxes.

Why the ratio is what matters—
Shape index depends only on the ratio of the two curvatures, never on their size. Double both k₁ and k₂ and the shape index does not move at all, while curvedness doubles. That is the whole point of splitting the pair this way: one number carries the size, the other carries the form, and you can ask about either without the other getting in the way.

3Curvedness: how hard is it bending?

One number, no shape information at all

From one point to a whole map. Steps 1 and 2 worked on a single point whose two curvatures you set by hand. From here on the same arithmetic runs at every sample of a modeled surface, shown in the header: a bowl in the north-west and a dome in the south-east, a north–south ridge below the bowl and a syncline above the dome, and a broad gentle swell underneath the lot which makes saddles wherever it meets the sharper features. One of every shape, so every component in step 5 has something to find.

Curvedness is the other half of the pair, and it throws the shape away completely. A sharp dome and an equally sharp bowl have identical curvedness, because both are bent just as hard — one up, one down. It is always positive or zero.

On its own that makes it a good deformation intensity map. Bright means the reflector is strongly bent, whatever way it happens to be bending, so faults, flexures and fold hinges all come out bright and gently dipping monoclines come out dark.

4Shape index on its own is misleading

The trap, and the standard fix

Here is the problem. Shape index is built from a ratio, and on a flat piece of reflector both curvatures are essentially zero, so the ratio is noise divided by noise. The attribute happily returns a confident-looking value of +0.5 or −1 for a patch of ground that has no shape whatsoever.

Displayed raw, a shape index map is therefore loud everywhere, and the loudest areas are often the flattest ones. The standard fix is to modulate it by curvedness, so that shape is only allowed to show where there is enough bend for the question to mean anything. Compare the two panels — the second is the same attribute with its brightness governed by curvedness.

5Shape components

One volume per shape

Rather than ask you to read a shape index map by eye, the shape can be split into five separate volumes. Each one is simply curvedness, kept where the shape index falls in that shape's band and zeroed everywhere else. So the dome volume is bright where the surface is strongly bent and dome-shaped, and blank everywhere else.

These are easy to interpret and easy to co-render, and they are the form most people end up using. Pick a shape below and see where on this surface it occurs.

?Why anyone bothers

What the shape is actually telling you about the rock

Everything so far has been geometry. Here is why an interpreter reaches for these two attributes rather than staying with k₁ and k₂.

Shape carries a process. The five shapes are not just descriptions — different geological processes make different shapes, so the shape index is a first, cheap guess at mechanism:

  • Bowls are collapse features. Karst sinkholes, dissolution of evaporites, collapsed reef cores, and the tops of chimneys all give closed circular lows. A bowl component map is one of the fastest ways to spot them, and they are worth spotting whether you are drilling through them or avoiding them.
  • Domes close in every direction, so they are the classic four-way structural trap, and also the signature of a buried mound, a reef, or a shallow gas pocket pushing a reflector up.
  • Ridges and valleys are elongated, so they follow a structural or depositional grain: fold hinges, fault-drag rollovers, differential compaction over a channel or a bar. A ridge over a channel is compaction, not structure, and knowing which shape you are looking at is the clue.
  • Saddles mark where two trends interfere. They are where fold axes cross, where a structure plunges between two culminations, and where the strain is most complicated — which is often where the fracturing is.

Curvedness carries intensity, and intensity relates to strain. Bending a brittle layer puts its outer arc into extension, so strongly curved zones are where open fractures are most likely to have formed. Curvedness is the attribute that answers "how hard has this rock been bent", independently of which way, which makes it a common input to fracture-density prediction and to picking where a horizontal well should be landed. Treat that as a correlation to be tested against well data, not a law — curvature is geometry, and fractures depend on lithology, burial history and the stress field as well.

And two numbers are easier to work with than two curvatures. Co-rendering k₁ and k₂ means reading two signed volumes at once. Curvedness modulated by shape index puts intensity in the brightness and mechanism in the color, which is one image instead of two, and the shape components turn it into a set of yes-or-no maps that can be handed to someone else without a tutorial.

The honest caveat: all of this is geometry computed from a dip field. A shape index map does not know about lithology, and a bowl in the attribute might be a sinkhole, a processing artifact, or a patch of bad velocities. The shape narrows the list of explanations; it does not close it.

Exercises

  1. Check that shape index ignores size. Set k₁ to +0.30 and k₂ to +0.10, note the shape index, then double both to +0.60 and +0.20.
    HintThe shape index does not move — it stays at +0.70 — while curvedness doubles from 0.22 to 0.45. Shape index is built from the ratio of the two curvatures, so any uniform scaling cancels out of it entirely. This is exactly what you want from a shape descriptor: a small gentle dome and a huge sharp dome are both domes, and one number should say so while a different number carries the difference in intensity.
  2. Walk the five landmarks. Click each preset in turn and write down the shape index.
    HintDome +1.00, ridge +0.50, saddle 0.00, valley −0.50, bowl −1.00, evenly spaced across the scale. The pattern is worth internalizing: positive means convex-up somewhere, negative means concave-up, and zero means the two curvatures are equal and opposite so the surface goes up one way and down the other. Anything between the landmarks is a blend, and real data is mostly blends.
  3. Break it on a flat surface. Set both k₁ and k₂ close to zero and read the shape index, then look at step 4.
    HintThe shape index still reports a value, and it will swing wildly for tiny changes in either curvature, because it is a ratio of two numbers that are both nearly zero. Look at the raw panel in step 4: it is fully saturated from corner to corner, including the broad quiet ground between the features where there is barely any curvature to speak of. The color there is not weak or uncertain looking, it is as strong as the color on the dome, and none of it means anything. This is the single most common way a shape index display misleads people, and it is why the modulated version is the one worth trusting.
  4. Find a saddle you would not have guessed at. In step 5, select the saddle component.
    HintSaddles turn up between adjacent highs, on the plunging noses of ridges, and around the rims of bowls — places you would not naturally call a saddle by eye, but which genuinely curve up one way and down the other. Saddles are far more common on real surfaces than the textbook diagram suggests, which is why a saddle component often looks busier than people expect the first time they run one — on this surface it claims 18% of the map, against 2.9% for the dome and 2.3% for the bowl.
  5. Compare curvedness with k₁. In step 3, find the bowl on both panels.
    HintOn the k₁ panel the bowl is dark, because k₁ is negative there. On the curvedness panel it is just as bright as the dome, because curvedness has thrown the sign away and only reports how hard the surface is bending. If you want deformation intensity, curvedness is the right choice. If you need to know whether something is arching up or sagging down, curvedness cannot tell you and you need the signed curvatures or the shape index.

The main points

  • Curvedness and shape index are k₁ and k₂ in polar form. Distance from the origin is how much it bends; direction is what shape it bends into.
  • Shape index ignores magnitude entirely. It is built from a ratio, so a gentle dome and a violent one give the same number.
  • Curvedness ignores shape entirely. A dome and a bowl of the same intensity are indistinguishable on it.
  • The five names are landmarks, not boxes. Dome +1, ridge +0.5, saddle 0, valley −0.5, bowl −1, with everything in between perfectly legitimate.
  • Never display shape index raw. On flat ground it is a ratio of noise to noise and returns confident nonsense. Modulate it by curvedness.
  • Shape components are the practical form. Curvedness gated by shape band, one volume per shape, easy to co-render and hard to misread.
Method, sources, and what has been simplified

The header sliders set k₁ and k₂ directly, so the first two steps are arithmetic on two numbers you control rather than anything extracted from data. The little surface patch is the quadratic z = ½(k₁x² + k₂y²), which is the surface those two principal curvatures describe near a point, drawn in oblique projection. The maps in steps 3 to 5 come from a modeled structure containing a dome, a ridge, a syncline and a bowl, with curvature computed the same way module 05 computes it.

Curvedness is C = √((k₁² + k₂²) / 2), always positive or zero. Shape index is s = (2/π)·tan⁻¹[(k₁ + k₂) / (k₁ − k₂)] with k₁ ≥ k₂, which gives +1 for a dome, +0.5 for a ridge, 0 for a saddle, −0.5 for a valley and −1 for a bowl. Those five values are checked numerically by the code rather than asserted. The shape components are curvedness kept inside a shape index band and zeroed outside it: bowl below −0.75, valley from −0.75 to −0.25, saddle from −0.25 to +0.25, ridge from +0.25 to +0.75, and dome above +0.75.

A discrepancy in the source, stated plainly. The AASPI curvature3d documentation gives curvedness twice, once as √(k₁² + k₂²) and once as √((k₁² + k₂²)/2). The second matches Roberts (2001) and is the one used here; the two differ by a factor of √2, which matters if you are comparing absolute numbers between packages and not at all if you are looking at a map. The shape index likewise appears in that document with two opposite sign conventions. The one used here is the one that puts domes at +1, which is the common convention, but it is worth checking which way round your own software has it before trusting the sign of a printed number.

Because shape index is a ratio, it is undefined where the surface is planar and both curvatures vanish. AASPI's approach, followed here, is to modulate the display by curvedness so that the shape is only visible where there is enough curvature to define it — and the shape components do the same thing by construction, since curvedness is what they are made of.

Simplifications. This is one surface rather than a volume. Everything here applies unchanged to amplitude curvature: the same curvedness and shape index are built from e_pos and e_neg in module 07, and AASPI outputs the same five shape components for them, prefixed e_.
