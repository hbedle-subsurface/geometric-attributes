Module 03  ·  Geometric attributes

Dip steering: why the window has to follow the bed

Modules 01 and 02 produced a reflector orientation at every sample. This module is about what that orientation is for. Every attribute that compares neighbouring traces has to decide where to look on each of them, and the difference between looking along the bed and looking straight across the screen decides what the attribute ends up imaging.

One continuous reflector, two ways of sampling it

Step 2  ·  When does the horizontal window fail?

It depends on the wavelet, not on the geology

The curve below sweeps the bed dip and plots both scores. The steered window stays at 1.0 the whole way, because a continuous reflector is continuous. The horizontal window falls away, and where it falls depends on how much time shift builds up across the aperture compared with the period of the wavelet.

The wavelet

A higher frequency means a shorter period, so less time shift is needed before the traces stop matching.

The window

A wider aperture accumulates more time shift from end to end, so it fails at a gentler dip.

Height matters much less here. The problem is lateral misalignment, and a taller window does not fix it.

Exercises

  1. Find the tolerance of an unsteered window. In step 2, leave the frequency at 30 Hz and five traces, and read the dip at which the flat window drops below 0.8. Then double the frequency and read it again. About 1.6 ms per trace at 30 Hz and about 0.8 at 60 Hz, so doubling the frequency halves the tolerance. At 25 m bins and 3000 m/s, 1.6 ms per trace is a geological dip of roughly 5.5 degrees. An unsteered coherence volume is therefore usable on almost nothing: gentle regional dip is already enough to break it.
  2. Check that the failure is about the wavelet, not the dip. Try several combinations of frequency and aperture in step 2, and each time read the "shift as a fraction of one period" box at the dip where the flat curve crosses 0.8. It lands between about 15 and 22 percent every time, whatever the frequency and whatever the aperture. That is the actual rule, and it is worth carrying around: a horizontal window survives only while the time shift accumulated across it stays under about a fifth of a period. Everything else follows from that, including why higher frequency data needs better dip estimates rather than fewer.
  3. Compare the two volumes as an interpreter would. In step 3, look at the steered and unsteered panels and ask which anomalies you would pick as faults. On the steered panel the bedding sits at 0.999 and the fault at 0.66, so the fault is the only dark feature and there is no overlap at all. On the unsteered panel the bedding averages 0.86 but drops to 0.70 on the steepest flanks, while the fault sits at 0.55. The gap between the fault and the worst bedding has shrunk from 0.34 to 0.14, and the dark fabric on the flanks is now within reach of the fault anomaly. Anyone auto-tracking this volume would extract the fold flanks as faults.
  4. Give the steering a small, plausible error. In step 4, set the error to 0.5 ms per trace and read the separation. Bedding coherence falls from 0.999 to about 0.98 and the separation from the fault narrows by roughly a tenth. That is a small error by the standards of module 01 — smaller than the effect of coarsening the dip search step to 2 ms — and it has already spent part of your fault contrast. Steering is not a switch that is either on or off; the quality of the dip volume is carried directly into every attribute computed from it.
  5. Push the error past the structure itself. Set the error to 2 ms per trace and compare it with the steepest dip in the section, shown in the readout. The steepest true dip is about 2 ms per trace, so an error of 2 is a hundred percent error, and bedding coherence collapses to about 0.70 — the same value the unsteered window gave on the flanks. Steering by a badly wrong dip is no better than not steering, and it is more dangerous, because the volume was labelled as dip-steered.

The main points

  • An unsteered window measures dip, not discontinuity. On a continuous dipping reflector it returns a low value, and that value has nothing to do with the geology being broken.
  • The tolerance is set by the wavelet. A horizontal window holds up only while the time shift across it stays under roughly a fifth of a period, which at ordinary frequencies means only a few degrees of dip.
  • Steering separates the fault from the fold. It does not make the fault anomaly stronger; it removes the competing anomaly on the flanks so the fault is the only thing left dark.
  • A dip-steered volume is only as good as its dip volume. Error in the dip field is spent directly out of the contrast you were trying to measure.
  • This is why dip comes first. Coherence, curvature, aberrancy and structure-oriented filtering all take the dip field as an input, and none of them can tell you it was wrong.
Method, assumptions, and where to read more

Steps 1 and 2 use a single reflector across a small patch of traces, built by convolving one reflection coefficient with a Ricker wavelet and shifting each trace by the bed dip. Steps 3 and 4 use a 140-trace section at 2 ms sampling containing a folded sequence of reflectors cut by a vertical fault of 18 ms throw at trace 89. The dip field is estimated with the discrete semblance search of module 01 rather than being supplied analytically, so the steered result carries whatever error that search makes.

The similarity measure used throughout is semblance, computed on the real trace over a window of 2K+1 samples and 2J+1 traces. AASPI program similarity3d computes its attributes from the analytic trace, meaning the data together with its Hilbert transform, which suppresses the artefact known as structural leakage near amplitude zero crossings. That refinement, and the several different definitions of coherence available, are the subject of the next module. The point being made here — that the window has to follow the bed — applies to every member of the family.

Real dip steering does not add a constant error the way step 4 does. Errors in a dip volume vary from place to place and are usually worst exactly where the structure is most complex, which is where the attribute is most needed. A constant bias is used here because it is readable; treat the numbers as showing the sensitivity, not as a model of how dip fields actually fail.
