Module 07  ·  Geometric attributes

Amplitude curvature: bending in brightness, not in structure

Module 05 differentiated the shape of the reflector twice. This one differentiates something else entirely: how bright the reflector is as you walk along it. A channel can be perfectly flat and still have edges, and no amount of structural curvature will ever find them. The amplitude changes sharply right there, and that change is a bend the same arithmetic can measure.

1Two completely different kinds of bend

Same reflector, two things that can vary along it

Walk along a reflector and two separate things can change. The reflector can go up and down — that is structure, and its second derivative is the structural curvature of module 05. Or the reflector can get brighter and dimmer while staying at exactly the same depth, and the second derivative of that is amplitude curvature.

The line in the header has both. There is a gentle fold, and there is a channel cut across it whose fill is dimmer than the surrounding rock. Notice that the channel has no structural expression at all — the reflector does not step or bend where the channel edge is, it simply goes quiet. A structural attribute is blind to it by construction.

The two curves are independent. Drag the fold relief to zero and the structure curve flattens while the amplitude curve is untouched. Drag the channel dimming to zero and the reverse happens. Real data has both at once, and the two families of attribute exist because no single measurement sees both.

2Amplitude extracted along the horizon

The surface the attribute is actually computed on

Structural curvature is computed on a surface of depth. Amplitude curvature is computed on a surface of amplitude: at every trace, follow the reflector to wherever it sits and record how strong it is there. That gives a map with the same footprint as the structure map but an entirely different quantity in it.

This is the step where the channel appears. On the structure map it is invisible. On the amplitude map it is the most obvious thing there.

1 Structure on the horizon depth, in meters

2 Amplitude on the horizon the same surface, a different measurement

Both maps look down on the same patch of ground. The left one knows where the reflector is; the right one knows how loud it is. Everything in this module is the second derivative of the map on the right, in the same way that module 05 worked on the map on the left.

3Differentiate it twice: e_pos and e_neg

Crests and troughs of brightness, not of structure

Take the amplitude map, find its gradient in the inline and crossline directions, then differentiate again. The two eigenvalues of that second derivative are the most-positive and most-negative amplitude curvatures, written e_pos and e_neg.

Look at where they land. The channel interior is dim but flat, so its second derivative is near zero and it comes out quiet. What lights up is the pair of edges, where the amplitude turns a corner. One edge is a crest of the amplitude surface and the other is a trough, so a channel prints as one bright lineament on e_pos beside one on e_neg — the same paired signature a fault gives in structural curvature, for exactly the same reason.

4Why there is no circle, and no k₁

The units will not allow it

Module 05 made a point of fitting a circle to the reflector: curvature was one over its radius, and the principal curvatures k₁ and k₂ were corrected for the dip the reflector already had. None of that survives here, and the reason is units.

A structural surface is length against length — meters of depth against meters of distance — so a circle drawn on it is a real circle with a radius in meters. An amplitude surface is amplitude against length. There is no meaningful circle to fit to something whose two axes are not the same kind of quantity, and there is no dip to correct for, because the vertical axis is not a direction in the ground at all. The AASPI documentation says exactly this and draws the same conclusion: revert to the simpler most-positive and most-negative curvature.

So the family of attributes is smaller. You get e_pos, e_neg and their strike, and from those the curvedness and shape index of module 06. You do not get k₁ and k₂ in any meaningful sense, and two structural attributes disappear entirely: reflector convergence and rotation about the normal both describe how a surface in the ground is oriented, and an amplitude surface is not in the ground.

?Why run it at all

The features it finds are the ones structure cannot show you

Every attribute up to module 06 measures the shape of the reflector. That works beautifully for faults and folds and finds nothing at all in a flat-lying stratigraphic feature, because there is nothing there to bend. Amplitude curvature is the answer to that gap: it differentiates how bright the reflector is instead of where it is.

What it is used for

  • Channel and fan margins on a flat horizon. Step 5 is the argument in two panels: set the fold relief to zero and structural curvature goes blank while amplitude curvature still maps both edges.
  • Facies and fluid boundaries. A change of lithology, porosity or fill changes reflection strength without moving the reflector. The boundary is an amplitude edge, so this is the family that sees it.
  • Diagenetic fronts and gas-charged patches, which are amplitude features with no structural expression whatever.
  • Co-rendering with structural curvature. The two together separate a feature that is structural, one that is stratigraphic, and one that is both — which a single volume cannot do.

Three things to know before trusting it

It maps outlines, not bodies. The dim interior of a channel is flat, so its second derivative is near zero and it comes out quiet — about 4 against 65 at the edge. If you want the body of the channel you want an amplitude map, or an envelope map — the strength of the reflection regardless of whether it is a peak or a trough. This attribute gives you the boundary instead.

It only sees abrupt change. Soften the channel edge from 1 trace to 4 and the edge and the interior become indistinguishable, because a gradational boundary has no sharp second derivative anywhere. A facies change that takes a few hundred meters to happen will not appear, however large the total amplitude contrast across it.

Amplitude is noisier than structure, and this squares it. Dip is a fitted, smoothed estimate; amplitude is read straight off the trace. Differentiating that twice makes amplitude curvature the most noise-sensitive attribute in this course after aberrancy, and it is equally sensitive to acquisition footprint, tuning and any amplitude-altering processing step. An anomaly here is only as trustworthy as the amplitudes underneath it, so it belongs on data whose amplitudes you actually believe.

And the same discipline as everywhere else: a bright lineament means the amplitude turned a corner. Whether that corner is a channel edge, a tuning effect or a footprint stripe is the interpretation, and the attribute does not know.

Exercises

  1. Flatten the structure completely. Set the fold relief to 0 and look at step 5.
    HintStructural curvature goes blank — there is nothing left to bend — while amplitude curvature is entirely unaffected and still maps both channel edges. This is the case that matters in practice: a channel or a fan on a flat-lying horizon is common, and a structural attribute has nothing to say about it. The reverse test works too: put the relief back and take the channel dimming to 0, and now the amplitude panel is the blank one.
  2. Find out what actually lights up at a channel. In step 3, compare the value at the channel edge with the value in the middle of the channel.
    HintThe middle of the channel reads about 4 against 65 at the edge, even though it is much dimmer than its surroundings. Amplitude curvature is a second derivative, so it responds to where the amplitude changes, not to where it is low. A broad dim area is flat in the second derivative and produces almost nothing. If you want to map the body of a channel you want an amplitude or envelope attribute; amplitude curvature maps its outline.
  3. Sharpen and soften the edge. Move the edge-sharpness slider from 1 to 10 traces and watch the e_pos value at the edge.
    HintAt 1 trace the edge reads about 124 against 0.1 in the middle of the channel — a thousand to one, and the two edges are razor sharp. At 2 traces it is 65 against 4. By 4 traces it is 23 against 22, and the distinction has collapsed: the channel no longer has a flat floor and two sharp walls, it is one broad smooth low, so its second derivative is spread right across it instead of concentrated at the boundaries. The lesson is not simply that the anomaly gets weaker. It is that amplitude curvature stops mapping an outline and starts mapping a blur, and a gradational facies boundary will never give you the crisp lineament a scoured edge does.
  4. Narrow the channel until it disappears. Take the channel width down toward 4 traces with the operator length at 7 or more.
    HintThe two edges move close enough together that the operator smooths across both at once and they begin to cancel, and the two clean lineaments merge into one weak smear. Compare it against the amplitude map in step 2, where the channel is still perfectly obvious. Operator length sets the smallest feature you can resolve, so a long operator chosen to suppress noise will also quietly erase the narrow features you may have been looking for — and nothing in the attribute warns you that it has.
  5. Add noise and differentiate it twice. Put the noise up to about 20% with the operator length at 1.
    HintThe edges disappear into speckle. Amplitude curvature is a second derivative of a quantity that is already noisier than structure — dip is a smoothed, fitted estimate, whereas amplitude is read straight off the trace — so it is more sensitive to noise than structural curvature is, not less. Raising the operator length recovers the edges, at the cost of the narrow features.

The main points

  • It is the same arithmetic on a different surface. Structural curvature differentiates depth twice; amplitude curvature differentiates amplitude twice. Everything else about the calculation is the same.
  • It finds features with no structural expression. A flat-lying channel, a fan edge, a diagenetic boundary — all invisible to structural curvature, all visible here.
  • It maps edges, not bodies. The dim interior of a channel is quiet because it is flat. The anomaly is at the boundary, where the amplitude turns a corner.
  • There is no circle and no k₁. The two axes are not the same kind of quantity, so a radius is meaningless and there is no dip to correct for. You get e_pos, e_neg and their strike, and nothing that depends on fitting a circle.
  • Convergence and rotation do not exist here. They describe the orientation of a surface in the ground, and an amplitude surface is not in the ground.
  • Run it alongside structural curvature, not instead of it. Each is blind to what the other measures.
Method, sources, and what has been simplified

The model is one reflector carrying a gentle fold, cut by a channel whose fill is dimmer than the surrounding rock. The channel has no structural expression whatever — the reflector runs through it at exactly the depth it would have had otherwise — so any response to it in a structural attribute would be an artifact. Depth and reflection strength are stored as two independent maps and the seismic line is built by convolving the second with a 30 Hz Ricker wavelet and shifting it by the first.

Amplitude curvature is computed exactly as structural curvature was in module 05, on a different input. The amplitude extracted along the horizon is differenced in the inline and crossline directions to give two gradients, those are smoothed along the operator length, and differenced again to give the three second derivatives. e_pos and e_neg are the two eigenvalues of that second-derivative matrix, and the strike is its eigenvector direction. There is no term in the dip components anywhere, which is the whole point of step 4.

Where this departs from AASPI, and why. Program curvature3d takes as input the inline and crossline components of the coherent energy gradients computed by program similarity3d, not amplitude read off a picked horizon. Coherent energy is the energy retained by the Karhunen–Loève filter of module 04, so it is a smoothed, dip-steered measure of reflection strength rather than a raw sample value, and using it makes the result markedly more stable. Amplitude along a horizon is used here because it is the version a beginner can see the whole of, and because the second-derivative behavior being taught is identical. AASPI also allows envelope or impedance as the input quantity.

The documentation is explicit about the two consequences drawn in step 4. Because the amplitude gradients carry units of amplitude per unit distance rather than length per unit length, it makes no sense to fit a circle tangent to the surface, so the computation reverts to most-positive and most-negative curvature, which highlight crests and troughs of amplitude without including the effects of structural dip. And reflector convergence and reflector rotation lose their meaning for amplitude curvature. There is also no vertical derivative, because the vertical axis is time or depth while the input derivatives are amplitude gradients.

Simplifications. This is one horizon rather than a volume. The operator is a smoothing length rather than the fractional-derivative operator AASPI uses, which its documentation describes as a first derivative followed by a low-pass filter; the wavelength behavior demonstrated is the same. Curvedness, shape index and the shape components are computed from e_pos and e_neg in exactly the way module 06 computes them from k₁ and k₂, and are covered there rather than repeated here.
