Module 03  ·  Geometric attributes

Dip steering: why the window has to follow the bed

Modules 01 and 02 produced a reflector orientation at every sample. This module is about what that orientation is for. Every attribute that compares neighboring traces has to decide where to look on each of them, and the difference between looking along the bed and looking straight across the screen decides what the attribute ends up imaging.

The wavelet and the window — these apply to every step

The wavelet

A higher frequency means a shorter period, so less time shift is needed before the traces stop matching.

The window

A wider aperture accumulates more time shift from end to end, so it fails at a gentler dip.

Height matters much less here. The problem is lateral misalignment, and a taller window does not fix it.

2When does the horizontal window fail?

It depends on the wavelet, not on the geology

Think of it as two lengths of time competing. The first is how much the reflector's arrival time changes from one end of the window to the other — the dip multiplied by the width of the aperture. The second is the period of the wavelet, the time from one peak to the next.

While the first is a small fraction of the second, the traces still broadly overlap and the window sees one event. As it grows toward half a period, the far end of the window is reading a trough where the near end reads a peak, and the window is being shown two opposite things and asked whether they agree. That is the whole mechanism, and it explains why the failure point depends on the wavelet rather than on anything geological.

Below are five snapshots of the same five traces, read straight across at five different bed dips. The reflector is perfectly continuous in every one of them — nothing is broken and nothing has changed but the dip. Watch the traces slide out of line, and watch the score fall as they do. A dip-steered window would show you the first picture every single time, whatever the dip.

Under each snapshot is the number that explains it: how much time shift has built up across the window, as a fraction of one period of the wavelet. That single quantity decides everything here.

The same comparison on a time slice

A section shows the fault as a line, which is a hard place to judge how much damage an unsteered window does. A map view is much clearer. These two panels are horizontal slices through a small 3D volume: a sequence of reflectors carried over a monoclinal flexure — a one-sided step in the structure, flat on both sides and steepening through the middle — cut by one planar fault. The fault is identical in both panels. Everything else on the right is the structure printing itself onto the attribute.

These two panels use a fixed patch of 3 by 3 traces, not the aperture set by traces used in the header; that slider drives the section panels above. Window height is shared. The values quoted in the exercises for these maps therefore refer to a three-trace window.

?Why steering is not optional

The difference between imaging geology and imaging the display

An unsteered attribute does not fail loudly. It produces a volume that looks entirely plausible, full of lineaments and fabric, and the lineaments are contours of the structure rather than anything broken. That is the danger: there is no error message, no obvious artifact, just a confident picture of the wrong thing.

What it changes in practice

  • Fault picking. On the unsteered volume in step 3, the darkest thing on a row is the fault only about half the time. Anyone auto-tracking it extracts fold flanks as faults over much of its length, and those picks look like perfectly good faults until someone checks them against the seismic.
  • Stratigraphic work. Bleed-through in map view follows the structural grain, which is exactly what makes it convincing as stratigraphy. The time slice in step 3 shows a broad band along the flexure that reads like a depositional feature and is nothing of the kind.
  • Every attribute above coherence. Curvature, shape index, amplitude curvature and aberrancy are all computed in the steered frame. The dip volume is not just an input to them; it is the coordinate system they are expressed in.
  • Structure-oriented filtering. Steered, it smooths along bedding and sharpens faults. Wrongly steered, it smooths across bedding, removing the discontinuities you were about to go looking for, and the output looks cleaner rather than worse.

And why the dip volume deserves real effort

Steering is not a switch that is either on or off. Step 4 shows a half-millisecond per trace error — smaller than the cost of coarsening the dip search step in module 01 — already eating into the fault contrast, and a 2 ms per trace error landing in the same place as not steering at all. Since the volume is still labeled dip-steered, that failure is harder to spot than the honest unsteered version.

Which is the practical argument for the order of this course. Modules 01 and 02 spend a long time on getting dip right, and this is the module that says why: the quality of the dip field is carried, undiminished, into everything computed from it.

One rule of thumb worth taking away: a horizontal window survives only while the time shift accumulated across it stays under about a fifth of a period. At ordinary frequencies that is a few degrees of dip, which almost no real survey manages.

Exercises

  1. Find the tolerance of an unsteered window. In step 2, leave the frequency at 30 Hz and five traces, and read the dip at which the flat window drops below 0.8. Then double the frequency and read it again.
    HintAbout 1.7 ms per trace at 30 Hz and about 0.9 at 60 Hz, so doubling the frequency roughly halves the tolerance. At 25 m bins and 3000 m/s those are geological dips of roughly six and three degrees. An unsteered coherence volume is therefore usable on almost nothing: gentle regional dip is already enough to break it, and better data makes it worse rather than better.
  2. Check that the failure is about the wavelet, not the dip. Try several combinations of frequency and aperture in step 2, and each time read the "shift as a fraction of one period" box at the dip where the flat curve crosses 0.8.
    HintIt lands between about 15 and 30 percent every time, averaging a little over a fifth. Sweeping frequency and aperture together moves the crossing dip over a range of nearly twenty to one — from several milliseconds per trace down to a few tenths — and every one of those crossings corresponds to much the same fraction of a period. That is the actual rule, and it is worth carrying around: a horizontal window survives only while the time shift accumulated across it stays under about a fifth of a period.
  3. Compare the two volumes as an interpreter would. In step 3, look at the steered and unsteered panels and ask which anomalies you would pick as faults.
    HintWith the noise slider at its default 12%, the steered panel sits at better than 0.9 on the bedding against roughly 0.5 at the fault, and the darkest point on a row is the fault about nine times in ten. The unsteered panel has bedding averaging under 0.8 and falling on the steepest flanks to about what the fault reads, and the darkest point is the fault only about half the time. So on many rows of the unsteered volume the darkest thing available to pick is a fold flank, not the fault. Turn the noise off and the steered background climbs to a flat 1.0 while the unsteered one still bands: the flanks are not a noise problem, they are a steering problem.
  4. Give the steering a small, plausible error. In step 4, step the error through 0.25, 0.5 and 1 ms per trace and watch the bedding value.
    HintBedding coherence falls steadily as the error goes 0, 0.25, 0.5, 1 ms per trace, losing the best part of a tenth by the end, while the fault value barely moves. The cost comes out of the background rather than out of the anomaly, so the separation between the two narrows by around a tenth of itself. These are small errors by the standards of module 01 — 0.5 ms per trace is less than the cost of coarsening the dip search step to 2 ms — and they are already being spent.
  5. Read the two time slices. In step 3, look at the map pair rather than the sections and ask what the broad dark band on the right is, and why it runs at a different angle from the fault.
    HintIt is the flexure. Wherever the bed dips more steeply than a three-trace horizontal window tolerates, the attribute darkens, so the feature it draws is the structure itself. The band goes about as dark as the fault does, so the two anomalies are comparable in strength and no threshold separates them. On the steered slice the background stays high across the whole map, flexure included, and the fault is the only linear feature on it. This is the clearest view of the problem, because in map view the bleed-through has a shape, and that shape looks like stratigraphy to anyone who has not seen the steered version.
  6. Push the error past the structure itself. Set the error to 2 ms per trace and compare it with the steepest dip in the section, shown in the readout.
    HintThe steepest true dip in this section is just under 2 ms per trace, so an error of 2 is a hundred percent error. Bedding coherence collapses to well below what the unsteered window managed, and the fault is the darkest point on a row less than half the time rather than nine times in ten. Steering by a badly wrong dip is therefore worse than not steering at all, and more dangerous, because the volume is labeled as dip-steered. Push on to 4 ms per trace and the fault is found on barely one row in ten: the attribute is imaging the error.

The main points

  • An unsteered window measures dip, not discontinuity. On a continuous dipping reflector it returns a low value, and that value has nothing to do with the geology being broken.
  • The tolerance is set by the wavelet. A horizontal window holds up only while the time shift across it stays under roughly a fifth of a period, which at ordinary frequencies means only a few degrees of dip.
  • Steering separates the fault from the fold. It does not make the fault anomaly stronger; it removes the competing anomaly on the flanks so the fault is the only thing left dark.
  • A dip-steered volume is only as good as its dip volume. Error in the dip field is spent directly out of the contrast you were trying to measure.
  • Height matters far less than aperture. The failure is lateral misalignment across the window, so a shorter window does not fix an unsteered one; a narrower one does.
  • This is why dip comes first. Coherence, curvature, aberrancy and structure-oriented filtering all take the dip field as an input, and none of them can tell you it was wrong.
Method, assumptions, and where to read more

Steps 1 and 2 use a single reflector across a small patch of traces, built by convolving one reflection coefficient with a Ricker wavelet and shifting each trace by the bed dip. Steps 3 and 4 use a 160-trace section at 2 ms sampling containing a gently folded sequence with a regional dip, cut by a normal fault of 20 ms throw whose plane descends 7.8 ms of two-way time per trace, about 25 degrees at 25 m bins and 3000 m/s. The reflectors are closely spaced, at 9 samples, for a reason: a fault is only visible where it cuts a reflector, so with widely spaced beds its expression becomes a row of isolated offsets several traces apart, which reads as a staircase rather than a plane. Because the fault plane is oblique to the reflectors it crosses each one at a different trace, which is why the coherence anomaly leans. Fault values quoted in the exercises are the darkest point found on each time row, which is what an interpreter or an autotracker responds to, rather than an average over a fixed band, and they assume the noise slider at its default 12%. The throw is applied with a sub-trace transition so that the fault plane is straight rather than quantized into a staircase of whole traces.

The two map panels come from a separate small 3D volume, 100 traces square by a slab of 81 samples, carrying a reflector sequence over a monoclinal flexure of 80 ms relief cut by one planar fault. The flexure reaches about 4 ms per trace at its steepest, above what a three-trace horizontal window tolerates at 30 Hz and below what a steered one minds at all. The two families of panel are steered differently, on purpose. The map panels take the analytic dip of the model, because estimation error is the subject of step 4 and mixing the two would confuse which effect is which. The section panels in steps 3 and 4 take a dip field estimated with the discrete semblance search of module 01, so the steered sections carry whatever error that search makes — which is what lets step 4 add an error on top of it and watch the cost.

The similarity measure used throughout is semblance, computed on the real trace over a window of 2K+1 samples and 2J+1 traces. AASPI program similarity3d computes its attributes from the analytic trace, meaning the data together with its Hilbert transform, which suppresses structural leakage: the false low-coherence stripe that appears where a reflector's amplitude passes through zero and the traces have almost nothing left to compare. That refinement, and the several different definitions of coherence available, are the subject of the next module. The point being made here — that the window has to follow the bed — applies to every member of the family.

Real dip steering does not add a constant error the way step 4 does. Errors in a dip volume vary from place to place and are usually worst exactly where the structure is most complex, which is where the attribute is most needed. A constant bias is used here because it is readable; treat the numbers as showing the sensitivity, not as a model of how dip fields actually fail.
