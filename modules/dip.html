Module 01  ·  Geometric attributes

Dip: the direction everything else is measured along

Rock layers are almost never flat. Beds tilt, fold, thin and break, and reflectors follow them. Any measurement made along a reflector therefore has to begin by establishing which way that reflector runs. This module covers it in three steps: what dip is, how you would estimate it by hand, and how the standard algorithm estimates it.

Converting the time shift into an angle requires a trace spacing and a velocity, here 25 m and 3000 m/s. The seismic data itself contains only the time shift.

Why dip is computed first

Geometric attributes are measured along the reflector rather than horizontally across the display. That requires knowing the reflector orientation in advance, and because bedding is rarely flat, the orientation differs from sample to sample.

A dip volume is therefore computed first: an estimate of bed dip at every sample, made without picking a horizon. The attributes listed opposite are then computed relative to it.

What depends on it

  • Coherence compares neighbouring traces along the bed. Compared horizontally instead, a continuous dipping reflector reads as a discontinuity, and the output images bedding rather than faults.
  • Curvature describes how quickly dip changes laterally. It is computed from the dip field, so its quality is limited by the quality of the dip estimate.
  • Aberrancy takes a further derivative, so any error in the dip field is amplified again.
  • Structure-oriented filtering smooths along bedding. Given the wrong orientation it smooths across reflectors instead, and the result still looks clean.

The highest score you can reach is rarely 1.0, and on field data it is much lower. Traces differ for reasons unrelated to dip, including noise, bed thinning and lateral changes in lithology. The aim is the best available match, not a perfect one.

Step 3  ·  Let the computer do it

Same data, same job, every tilt tried in turn

The algorithm cannot judge by eye whether the line follows a bed, but it can do the rest of what you just did: sample the traces along a tilted line, measure how well they agree, and record the result. It repeats this for every tilt in a range and keeps the highest-scoring one. The estimate it returns is the tilt that best aligns the traces, which is what correcting for bed dip amounts to in practice.

The number of traces compared at once. Three gives very little evidence to work from; twenty-one spans 250 m either side of the point being described.

The length of trace compared. A common guideline is about one wavelength, roughly ±17 ms at 30 Hz. At ±100 ms the window spans three separate reflectors.

Exercises

  1. Search too narrow a range. Press "tell me the answer" so the target is visible, then reduce the search range until the real dip falls outside it. The estimate stops at the edge of the range and stays there. The bars are still rising when the search runs out of tilts to try. No warning is produced, and the value returned looks no different from a correct one. This is the most common cause of a bad dip volume.
  2. Coarsen the step size. Set the step size to 2 ms/trace, then switch "fine-tune the winner" off and on several times. With it off, the estimate can only take one of the nine values tested, so it moves in jumps and averages roughly four times further from the correct answer. With it on, the parabola fit recovers most of that precision at almost no computational cost, which is what allows production software to search coarsely. At a 4 ms step the fit begins to fail: a parabola needs the peak to be sampled, and five tilts across the range do not sample it.
  3. Widen the window across a fault. Switch the fault on and change nothing else; at nine traces the window still clears it. Now increase "traces used" to 21. The score drops from about 0.98 to about 0.51 as soon as the tilted box reaches across the break, and the winning tilt becomes a compromise between two beds that are not the same bed. The fault is one trace wide, but the estimate is degraded for four traces either side of it, because that is how far the window extends. Coherence computed on a dip field smeared this way produces broad, indistinct faults. Production software addresses this by testing several windows offset around the sample and keeping the most confident, rather than always centring the window.
  4. Make the window too tall. Switch on "bed dip changes with depth", then raise the window height from ±30 ms to ±100 ms, watching the box extend into the lower beds. Little changes until about ±80 ms, after which the score falls from 0.98 to around 0.70 and the estimate moves toward an average of two unrelated dips. Switch the depth change off and repeat: the same window heights now cost nothing. The data did not become harder to work with; the window was simply told to treat a larger interval as one unit. Window height is really a statement about how far the geology can be assumed constant, which is why it is normally quoted as a fraction of a wavelength.
  5. Reduce the number of traces and compare the score with the error. Set noise to about 30%, then lower "traces used" from 9 to 3, reading both figures as you go. The score rises, from around 0.68 to around 0.85, while the estimate becomes roughly four times worse. Three traces have less scope to disagree, so they match more easily and establish much less. A high score is therefore not evidence of a good estimate, and a confidence volume has to be interpreted alongside the window size that produced it.
  6. Increase the noise until the estimate fails. Raise noise in stages to 20%, 40% and 60%, pressing "new hidden dip" several times at each level. The estimate degrades gradually: barely affected at 20%, a few tenths of a millisecond out at 40%. Past 50% the bars lose their single clear peak and the winner sometimes settles on the wrong one. The score falls well before the estimate does, from 0.99 to below 0.6. That difference is the reason attribute software writes a confidence volume alongside the dip: it registers the problem while the dip values still look plausible.

The main points

  • Dip is the normal condition of a reflector, not a special case reserved for structured areas.
  • Dip is a time shift per trace. Converting it to an angle requires a trace spacing and a velocity, both supplied by the interpreter rather than measured.
  • The estimate is obtained by trial, not by measurement. Every permitted tilt is tested, scored on how well the traces align along it, and the best is kept.
  • A low score is a warning, but a high score is not a guarantee. Fewer traces and shorter windows both raise the score while worsening the estimate, because less evidence is available to contradict it.
  • The window must be small enough for the geology inside it to be uniform. Too wide and it crosses a fault; too tall and it reaches beds dipping the other way.
  • Later attributes inherit the result. Coherence is steered by it, curvature is computed from it, aberrancy differentiates it again. Check the dip field before attributing a problem to the attribute.
Method, assumptions, and where to read more

Twenty-five traces sampled every 2 ms, formed by convolving a set of reflection coefficients with a 30 Hz Ricker wavelet and shifting each trace according to the dip. The hidden dip is drawn from a seeded random number, so "new hidden dip" produces a different case each time while a shared link reproduces the same one. The optional fault is a vertical step in arrival time applied to every trace from trace 17 rightward. The optional depth change reverses the dip of every bed below about 290 ms.

The score is semblance: the energy of the summed traces divided by the summed energy of the individual traces, over the analysis window. It is 1 when every trace in the window is identical and falls toward 1/N when they are unrelated. Marfurt et al. (1998) introduced it as a coherence measure, and it is also the quantity the dip search maximises. Coherence and dip estimation are the same calculation used to answer two different questions.

The search is the discrete dip scan of Marfurt et al. (1998), offered today as one of the two algorithms in AASPI program dip3d. Candidate tilts are spaced evenly across the range, each trace is read along the tilted line with linear interpolation between samples, and the winner is refined by fitting a parabola through it and its two neighbours.

Several simplifications, each treated in a later module. This is 2D, so there is a single dip component rather than the inline and crossline pair, and no dip azimuth. The search range is quoted in milliseconds per trace, whereas production software asks for it in degrees and requires a conversion velocity. Semblance is computed on the plain trace rather than the analytic trace. Dip is estimated at a single point rather than at every sample in a volume. There is no Kuwahara search over shifted windows, which is what keeps a production dip estimate from smearing across a fault.
