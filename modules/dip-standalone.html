Dip: the direction everything else is measured along

At every single sample in a seismic volume there is a local reflector orientation. Nothing in your data announces it — it has to be estimated, by an algorithm, with parameters you chose or inherited. Coherence, curvature, aberrancy and structure-oriented filtering are then all computed relative to that estimate. This module builds a structure, runs the estimate in front of you one candidate at a time, and then lets you break it.

What dip is

Take any sample in a seismic section. Ask a simple question: if I step one trace to the right, how much later or earlier does this same reflector arrive? That number — a time shift per trace — is the dip. In 2D it is one number. In 3D it is two, an inline component p and a crossline component q, which together give a dip magnitude and a dip azimuth.

Notice what dip is not. It is not picked from a horizon, so it does not need an interpretation and does not stop at the edge of one. It is computed at every sample, including inside packages nobody has picked and in the middle of noise. That is why it is called volumetric dip, and it is why a dip volume exists before any other geometric attribute does.

Dip is also an attribute in its own right. Co-render dip azimuth against dip magnitude on a time slice and the structure draws itself: flanks, noses, closures and fault blocks separate by colour without a single pick.

Why it has to come first

  • Coherence is measured along dip, not across it. Steer the window wrong and you compare a peak on one trace against a trough on the next. A perfectly continuous dipping reflector then reads as a discontinuity, and your coherence volume images the bedding instead of the faults.
  • Curvature is the derivative of the dip field. Aberrancy is the derivative after that. Differentiation amplifies error. A dip field that is 1° noisy is a curvature volume that is unusable, and the noise looks like structure rather than like noise.
  • Structure-oriented filtering smooths along dip. If the dip estimate locks onto coherent noise — migration smiles, ground roll, acquisition footprint — the filter will faithfully enhance the noise and suppress the signal. This failure is quiet, and the output looks cleaner.
  • The confidence volume is free QC. The winning semblance comes out of the search whether you ask for it or not. It falls at faults and in poor data, which tells you both where the dip is untrustworthy and, usefully, where the faults are.

Try this — and what to watch for

  1. Starve the search. Why bother: the search range is the parameter most often left at its default, and the default is a guess about your survey. Put the analysis point on the steepest part of a flank and drag the search range down to ±5°. The estimate pins at the edge of the fan and stops moving. The semblance curve is still climbing when it runs out of candidates. This failure is silent: the dip volume looks plausible, it is simply wrong wherever the structure was steeper than you allowed for, and the error is systematic rather than random — the worst kind for a derivative attribute.
  2. Coarsen the increment. Why bother: it is the cheapest way to halve your run time, and it is not free. Set the increment to 8°, then turn parabolic refinement off and on. With refinement off, the dip field goes visibly banded — the estimate can only take the values you offered it. With refinement on, fitting a parabola through the winner and its two neighbours recovers most of the precision back. This is why the AASPI search can afford a coarse fan.
  3. Put the window on the fault. Why bother: this is where dip estimates fail on every real survey. Drag the analysis point onto the fault, then widen the window radius to 5 traces. Semblance collapses and the winning dip becomes a compromise between two unrelated reflectors. The confidence panel goes black in a stripe down the fault. Widen the window and the stripe gets fatter — you have smeared the fault. This is the problem the Kuwahara shifted-window scheme in dip3d exists to solve: try several offset windows and keep the most confident one, rather than the centred one.
  4. Break the conversion. Why bother: dip in degrees is not measured, it is converted. Leave everything else alone and drag the conversion velocity from 3000 to 6000 m/s. The data did not change and the estimate in ms/trace did not change, but the reported angle halves — and so does the search range in samples, so a range that was generous becomes too narrow. A time-migrated volume converted with a single constant velocity carries this error into curvature, reflector convergence and aberrancy. It does not affect dip-steered computations such as coherence or structure-oriented filtering, which only need the time shift.
  5. Make the window too tall. Why bother: vertical window height is the quietest resolution knob in the whole workflow. Turn on the angular unconformity and drag the window half-height to ±28 ms. Watch the error panel rather than the dip panel. The band of wrong dip along the discordance thickens as the window grows, because the window is averaging two packages that have nothing to do with each other over a taller and taller interval. The dip either side of the band stays fine, which is exactly what makes this easy to miss. Rule of thumb: the full vertical window should be about one dominant period — at 30 Hz that is 33 ms, so ±16 ms.
  6. Turn up the noise, then look at the last row. Why bother: it separates a bad attribute from a bad dip field. Push noise to 40% and compare the two coherence panels. Both degrade, but the steered one degrades differently: where the dip estimate went wrong, coherence goes dark along the bedding, and that dark fabric will be interpreted as faults by anyone who did not compute the dip themselves. Noise in a dip volume does not stay in the dip volume.

If you remember nothing else

  • Dip is an estimate, not a measurement. It is the winner of a search you parameterised. Two reasonable people running two reasonable parameter sets get two different dip volumes.
  • The search range must cover your steepest structure. Everything steeper is clipped, quietly and systematically.
  • Window height trades noise against resolution; window radius trades noise against faults. Bigger is smoother, smoother is smeared, and smeared is what your fault looks like afterwards.
  • Confidence is the volume nobody looks at. It is the honest answer to "should I believe this dip here", and it comes out of the run for free.
  • Time dip is what the data knows; degrees is what you asked for. The conversion needs a velocity, and a wrong one is invisible in every display.
  • Every downstream attribute inherits this. Coherence steers by it, curvature differentiates it, structure-oriented filtering smooths along it. Fix the dip before blaming the attribute.
Method, assumptions, and where to read more

The structure is built as a set of reflection coefficients whose arrival time is displaced by a shift function: a linear regional term, a sinusoidal fold, an optional vertical step at trace 111 standing in for a fault, and an optional undeformed package above 0.17 s that truncates the fold and makes an angular unconformity. Each trace is the convolution of that reflectivity with a Ricker wavelet. Because the shift function is analytic, its derivative — the true dip at every sample — is known exactly, which is what the error panel compares against. Nothing on a real survey provides this.

Dip is estimated by the discrete semblance search of Marfurt et al. (1998), the same algorithm offered as the second choice in AASPI program dip3d. A fan of candidate time dips is generated across the search range; for each, the traces of the analysis window are read at a shift proportional to their distance from the centre, with linear interpolation, and semblance is computed over the window. The candidate with the highest semblance wins, then a parabola through it and its two neighbours refines the estimate off the candidate grid.

Where this departs from dip3d, and why. It is 2D, so there is one dip component rather than the inline and crossline pair, and no dip azimuth — that is the next module. Semblance is computed on the real trace rather than the analytic trace, so it is slightly less stable near zero crossings. Candidates are spaced evenly in time dip rather than on the polar angle grid dip3d has used since 2016. There is no Kuwahara search over shifted windows, no short-window AGC, no Tukey taper on the vertical window, and no L1-norm option — each of those is a real improvement that would hide the plain algorithm underneath. And dip here is computed on a decimated grid and interpolated for display, because a full-density scan at every slider movement would stutter; the decimation is stated in the readout and is a display choice, not part of the method.

The last row computes semblance coherence, not the energy-ratio similarity that program similarity3d produces. Semblance is used because it is already there — it is the same quantity the dip scan maximises — and because the point being made is about steering, which is common to every member of the coherence family.
