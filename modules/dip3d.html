Module 02  ·  Geometric attributes

Inline and crossline dip: one reflector, two numbers

In module 01 we estimated dip along a single seismic line by finding the tilt that best aligned neighbouring traces. A 3D volume allows the same measurement in two directions, and it turns out one direction is not enough to describe where a reflector is going. This module is about what changes between a line and a volume.

Step 4  ·  Putting the two components together

A time slice shows both at once

A time slice cuts the volume horizontally. Where the reflector crosses that time it leaves a band, and the bands run along the direction in which travel time does not change. Perpendicular to them is the direction in which it changes fastest. Those two directions are the strike and the dip direction, and they are set by the two numbers you have been adjusting.

Magnitude is not the sum of the two components, and it is not the larger of them. It is √(∂t/∂x)² + (∂t/∂y)², and the components have to be converted to the same units first. Inline dip is quoted per trace and crossline dip per line, so if the bins are not square those two are not comparable quantities.

Step 6  ·  Let the computer do it

The fan of candidate tilts becomes a grid

In module 01 the algorithm tried every tilt along a line and kept the best. Exercises

  1. Make a dipping bed look flat. Set inline dip to 0 and crossline dip to 6, then read the inline section in step 1. The inline section shows a perfectly horizontal reflector while the bed is dipping about 20 degrees. Nothing in that section is wrong or noisy; it is simply cut along strike, and along strike there is no time change to see. An interpreter working a single line through this volume would report zero dip and be describing the section correctly and the geology not at all.
  2. Find the two azimuths that matter. With inline 2 and crossline 4, use step 5 to find the azimuth where the section is steepest and the azimuth where it is flat. Steepest at about 63 degrees from inline, flat at about 153 degrees, and the two are exactly 90 degrees apart. The steepest direction is the dip azimuth and equals the arctangent of the two components; the flat direction is strike. Every other section shows something in between, following a cosine. This is why apparent dip is not a property of the reflector but of the reflector and the section together.
  3. Break the azimuth with rectangular bins. In step 4 set the inline bin to 12.5 m and leave the crossline bin at 25 m. Not one pixel of the sections or the time slice changes, because the volume is stored by bin number and the reflector still crosses the same number of traces. The computed azimuth moves from 63 degrees to 45, a swing of 18 degrees, because a step of one trace is now half the distance a step of one line is. Anything that combines the two components has to divide by the bin spacing first. The error is invisible on square-bin surveys, which is how it survives into production code.
  4. Compare a coarse grid with a coarse fan. In step 6, note the number of combinations at a 1 ms step, then set the step to 0.5 ms. The count goes from 289 to 1089, close to four times as many, because the grid is two-dimensional. In module 01, halving the step doubled the work. This is the practical reason production dip estimators do not simply search a fine grid, and why several use a gradient structure tensor instead, which solves for the orientation directly rather than trying candidates.
  5. Watch the peak spread before the answer moves. Raise noise to 30% and look at the score surface rather than the numbers. The bright spot broadens into a ridge well before the estimate becomes visibly wrong, and the score falls from 1.00 to about 0.75. The ridge is elongated, meaning the search is more certain about one combination of the components than the other. Averaged over several orientations, the joint 2D search is about a third more accurate at this noise level than running two separate 1D searches, one per direction, because it uses all the traces in the patch for both components at once.
  6. Reduce the patch to 3 × 3. With noise at 30%, drag the patch size down from 11 × 11 and read the score alongside the error. The score hardly moves: about 0.75 at 11 × 11 and about 0.77 at 3 × 3. The error changes by a factor of eighteen, from roughly 0.04 ms to roughly 0.70 ms per trace. Nine traces support two unknowns rather than one, and they span only a single trace either side of the analysis point in each direction, which is barely enough to define a plane. The score is not measuring how well determined the estimate is; it is measuring how well the traces inside the window happen to agree, and a small window makes that easy. Two volumes with the same confidence value can differ enormously in how much they should be trusted.

The main points

  • A reflector in a volume needs two numbers, not one. Inline dip and crossline dip are components of its orientation, and neither on its own is "the dip".
  • Apparent dip belongs to the section, not the reflector. The same bed shows a different dip in every vertical section, following a cosine that peaks at the dip azimuth and reaches zero along strike.
  • A section can be cut along strike through a steep bed and show nothing. Zero dip on one line is not evidence of a flat reflector.
  • Combining the components requires common units. Convert per trace and per line to per metre using the bin spacing before taking a magnitude or an azimuth.
  • Time dip is still not geological dip. The conversion needs a velocity, and the volume does not contain one.
  • The search is the same idea, one dimension larger. Align a patch of traces instead of a row, score it the same way, and accept that the cost grows as the square.
Method, assumptions, and where to read more

The volume is 25 inlines by 25 crosslines by 220 samples at 2 ms. The reflector is defined analytically as t(x, y) = t0 + px·x + py·y, and the amplitude at each sample is a 30 Hz Ricker wavelet evaluated at the offset from that surface, so the geometry is exact rather than interpolated. The optional second and third beds are parallel copies 70 ms above and below.

Azimuth here is measured in degrees clockwise from the inline axis and points downdip. Real surveys are rotated relative to north, so a dip azimuth volume has to be corrected by the survey rotation before it means anything geographic, and packages differ over whether azimuth points updip or downdip and whether it is measured from north or from the inline. Check the convention before comparing two volumes.

The rotated section in step 5 is sampled at a fixed 15 m along the line, using bilinear interpolation between the four surrounding traces. That fixed spacing is a display choice. In a real volume the natural trace spacing along a section varies with azimuth — a section cut diagonally across square bins has traces √2 further apart than one cut along the inline — which is a further reason apparent dip is quoted per unit distance rather than per trace once you leave the acquisition directions.

The search is the discrete scan of Marfurt et al. (1998) extended to two components, which is one of the two algorithms in AASPI program dip3d. Refinement fits a parabola along each axis through the winning cell. A true two-dimensional peak wants a paraboloid including a cross term; the separable form used here is what most implementations do and is accurate while the peak is not strongly skewed, which the elongated ridge under noise shows it can be.

Simplifications carried over from module 01, plus new ones. Dip is estimated at a single point rather than at every sample. Semblance is computed on the plain trace rather than the analytic trace. There is no Kuwahara search over shifted windows. The reflector is planar, so curvature is zero everywhere and the estimate is not fighting a changing orientation inside the window. And the alternative family of estimators, which builds a gradient structure tensor and takes its principal eigenvector rather than searching candidates, is not shown here at all.
