Module 05  ·  Geometric attributes

Curvature: how fast the dip is changing

Modules 01 and 02 measured which way the reflector is going. Curvature asks the next question: how fast is that direction changing as you move along the bed? Flat beds and uniformly dipping beds both have zero curvature — it is only where the dip changes that anything happens. That makes curvature a derivative of a derivative, which is where both its usefulness and its sensitivity to noise come from.

1From dip to curvature

One profile, differentiated twice

The seismic line above the tabs is the whole of the input. It runs west to east through a gentle fold and one small fault, and it stays on screen for every step so you can always see what the numbers are describing. The three curves below line up with it trace for trace. You have already built two of them, so let us take them in the order you met them.

  • Dip came first, in modules 01 to 03: which way the reflector is heading at every sample. On this line it is largest on the flanks of the fold and passes through zero at the crest and the trough.
  • Coherence came next, in module 04. It compares neighboring traces along that dip and asks whether they still look alike. It goes dark at the fault, where the reflector is broken. Across the fold it sees nothing whatever, because a fold is perfectly continuous: nothing is broken, the beds simply bend.
  • Curvature is the next step in the same chain — the rate at which the dip is changing. It lights up across the fold that coherence ignored, and it also reacts at the fault.

So the three attributes form a sequence rather than a menu. Dip tells you the orientation. Coherence asks whether the reflector is broken. Curvature asks whether it is bent. Coherence finds breaks; curvature finds bends, and a fault too small to break the reflector will still bend it. That is the gap curvature was brought in to fill.

Curvature has a pleasingly physical definition. At any point on the reflector, fit the circle that best hugs the bed. Curvature is k = 1/r, one over that circle's radius: a tight fold is a small circle and a big number, a gentle warp is a huge circle and a small one, and a straight bed is a circle of infinite radius, so k = 0. The sign follows the shape — anticlines positive, synclines negative, and zero on any plane, however steeply that plane dips.

Now try the fault-throw slider in the header. Bring it down to 5 m and watch the two curves part company: coherence returns to a flat 0.99, exactly as if there were no fault at all, while curvature still marks the spot. Below the seismic resolution the offset stops being a break and becomes a flexure — and a flexure is precisely what curvature measures. That is why a fault interpretation is normally run on coherence and curvature together, and why very small faults appear on one and not the other.

Finally, look at where the curvature peaks sit relative to the seismic. They are not on the steep flanks — the flanks are where curvature crosses zero. The peaks are at the crest and the trough, exactly where the reflector is momentarily flat. A bright curvature anomaly means a place where the dip is changing quickly, not a place that is steep, and that catches almost everyone out the first time.

The sign convention—
Curvature is quoted here in reciprocal kilometers, which is what you get when you fit a circle to the reflector and take one over its radius. A curvature of 1 km⁻¹ means a fold you could fit inside a circle of 1 km radius; 0.1 km⁻¹ is a gentle 10 km-radius warp. Anticlines are positive and synclines negative, which is the usual convention but not a universal one.

2Curvature depends on which way you cut

The same problem apparent dip had, one derivative later

Module 02 showed that apparent dip belongs to the section, not the reflector. Curvature has exactly the same problem, and it is worse, because a surface can curve one way in one direction and the other way at ninety degrees to it — a saddle. Cut a profile through the marked point at any azimuth and it has a curvature of its own.

Rotate the profile below and watch the number swing. It reaches a maximum in one direction and a minimum ninety degrees away, and those two extremes are what get called the principal curvatures, k₁ and k₂.

The two extremes are always ninety degrees apart, which is not obvious but is a genuine result about smooth surfaces. Euler's theorem then gives the curvature at any other azimuth from those two alone: k(φ) = k₁cos²(φ − ψ) + k₂sin²(φ − ψ), where ψ is the direction k₁ points. So the whole curve on the right is determined by two numbers and an angle.

3k₁ and k₂ across the whole map

Two volumes, and the strike that goes with them

Now compute those two numbers at every point rather than one. Most-positive curvature picks out anticlines, ridges and the upthrown edges of faults; most-negative picks out synclines, valleys and the downthrown edges. Run them together and a fault shows as a paired bright and dark lineament, which is the signature interpreters look for.

4Most-positive curvature is not k₁

Two attributes with confusingly similar names

Software offers both k₁ and something called k_pos, and they are not the same thing. The principal curvatures fit a circle to the reflector, which means correcting for how steeply the reflector is already dipping. The most-positive and most-negative curvatures skip that correction and are, in the words of the AASPI documentation, better read as a second-derivative map of structure than as a curvature.

On flat-lying structure the two agree almost exactly. Add regional dip with the slider in the header and watch them separate — k_pos hardly moves while k₁ falls. Tilting the whole surface does not change its second derivatives, so k_pos, which is built from those alone, barely changes. k₁ does, because fitting a circle to a dipping bed measures the bend along the bed rather than against the vertical, and a dipping bed is longer than its map projection. That foreshortening is the dip correction. The practical consequence is that on dipping structure k_pos reads systematically high.

The fold is identical on both sides. All that has changed is that the right-hand one sits on a tilt.

5Long and short wavelength

The one parameter that decides what you see

Differentiating twice has an unwelcome consequence: it multiplies the high wavenumbers twice over, so any short-wavelength wobble in the dip field comes out amplified. The usual sources are acquisition footprint, a regular pattern left by the shot and receiver geometry; statics, small trace-to-trace time shifts introduced by the near surface; and migration noise. That is why curvature is computed with an operator length rather than a bare difference, and why the same volume computed two ways looks like two different pieces of geology.

Turn on the short-wavelength texture and the noise in the header, then move the operator length. A short operator picks out the fine fabric; a long one shows only the broad fold. Neither is the right answer — they are answers to different questions, and choosing between them is an interpretation.

?Why bother with a third attribute

What curvature finds that dip and coherence cannot

Step 1 made the case in one picture: coherence needs a break and curvature needs only a bend. Below the seismic resolution a fault stops breaking the reflector and starts flexing it, and that is where curvature takes over. Walk the fault throw down in step 1 and coherence returns to its no-fault value at 5 m while curvature is still marking the spot.

What it is used for

  • Subtle faults and flexures. The ones too small for coherence, which are often the ones that matter for compartmentalization — a 5 m offset can still juxtapose sand against shale.
  • Fracture prediction. Bending a brittle layer puts its outer arc into extension, so strongly curved zones are where open fractures are most likely. This is a correlation to be tested against image logs and production, not a law, but it is the standard first-pass map for a fractured reservoir.
  • Differential compaction. A channel, a reef or a fan compacts differently from the mud around it, and drapes the overlying reflectors into a gentle ridge or sag. Curvature maps that drape cleanly, so you can see a body you cannot see directly.
  • Karst and collapse. Sinkholes and collapsed chimneys show as tight closed lows, which is exactly the shape module 06's bowl component isolates.
  • Structural framework. Most-positive and most-negative run together give the paired bright and dark lineament that reads as a fault, with the upthrown and downthrown sides labeled for you.

The three ways it will mislead you

Bright does not mean steep. Curvature peaks where the bed is momentarily flat, at a crest or a trough, and passes through zero where the bed is steepest — because a steeply dipping bed at its inflection is momentarily straight, and straight means not bending. On the default structure that is 0.013 km⁻¹ at the steepest point against 0.913 at the crest, a factor of seventy the other way from most people's intuition.

Operator length is an interpretation, not a setting. Long-wavelength curvature maps folds and short-wavelength curvature maps fabric, and the same surface honestly gives both. Which one you chose should travel with the volume, because nothing in the data says which is right.

It inherits everything the dip field got wrong, doubled. Curvature is a second derivative, so short-wavelength error in dip is amplified twice over. Put the noise slider to 30% at an operator length of 3 and the typical curvature reads six times its clean value, almost all of it noise. That is the practical reason modules 01 to 03 spent so long on getting dip right.

And the naming trap worth carrying away: k₁ and k_pos are different attributes, not two names for one. On dipping structure the uncorrected version reads systematically high, and it reads highest where the structure dips most steeply, so the bias does not average out.

Exercises

  1. Shrink the fault until only curvature can see it. In step 1, walk the fault throw down from 60 m to 5 m, reading the coherence value and the fault curvature at each stop.
    HintAt 60 m throw coherence reads 0.36 and the fault is unmistakable. At 25 m it is 0.84. At 10 m it is 0.97, and at 5 m it is 0.992 — which is exactly the value it reads with no fault at all. Coherence has gone completely blind. Curvature meanwhile goes 8.30, 3.96, 1.75, 0.98 km⁻¹: reduced, but still clearly there at every step. Once the offset drops below what the wavelet can resolve, the reflector is no longer broken, it is bent, and only an attribute that measures bending will find it. This is the classic progression — large offsets to coherence, small offsets to curvature, and offsets below even that to aberrancy, which has a module of its own. Notice too that the fold sits at 0.91 km⁻¹ throughout and coherence never sees it at any setting.
  2. Find where curvature is zero on a steeply dipping bed. In step 1, set the fold relief to about 100 m and look at where the middle and bottom panels cross zero.
    HintThe dip curve and the curvature curve cross zero in completely different places, a quarter of a wavelength apart. On the default structure, curvature at the steepest point on the fold is 0.013 km⁻¹ against 0.913 km⁻¹ at the crest — a factor of seventy. Curvature is zero exactly where the bed is steepest, and largest where the bed is level at a crest or a trough. The reason is worth saying carefully: at the steepest point the bed is momentarily straight, not level. It is dipping hard, but it is not bending, and bending is the only thing curvature measures. This is the single most common misreading of a curvature volume: a bright curvature anomaly is not a steep place, it is a place where the dip is changing quickly.
  3. Find a saddle. In step 2, click near the plunging nose at the north end of one of the ridges, then rotate the profile through 180°.
    HintThe curve crosses zero twice, and k₁ comes out positive while k₂ comes out negative at the same point — around +0.15 and −0.12 km⁻¹ at the strongest saddle on this surface. The reflector arches up along the ridge and falls away along its plunge, so it curves one way in one direction and the other way at right angles. No single number describes that, which is the whole reason two principal curvatures are reported instead of one average. A mean curvature here would be close to zero and would tell you nothing at all. Saddles are common on real surfaces, and on this modeled one they cover more than half the map.
  4. Separate k_pos from k₁. In step 4, start with the regional dip at 0° and raise it to 25°, watching the ratio readout.
    HintAt 0° the two agree to within a percent. By 25° k_pos reads about 1.22 times k₁. Notice which one moves: k_pos hardly moves while k₁ falls. Adding a tilt does not change a second derivative, and k_pos is nothing but second derivatives. k₁ falls because it measures the bend along the bed, and a bed dipping at 25° is longer than the map distance it covers, so the same rise is spread over more reflector. Dividing that foreshortening out is what the dip correction does, and it is why k_pos reads high wherever the structure dips. On a structurally dipping survey that difference is systematic rather than random, so it does not average out — it biases the whole map in the direction of steepest regional dip. If you are comparing curvature between two areas of a survey that dip differently, this matters.
  5. Watch the second derivative amplify noise. Set the operator length to 1 in the header, then put the dip-field noise up to about 15%.
    HintThe fold almost disappears under speckle. Now take the operator length up to 15 and the fold returns while the speckle goes. At an operator length of 1 the typical curvature reads about 16 times what the same surface gives at 15 traces, and almost all of that is noise rather than geology. Noise in a dip field is not a cosmetic problem for curvature — differentiating twice multiplies short-wavelength content twice over, so a dip volume that looked acceptable in module 01 can be unusable once it has been differentiated. This is the practical reason modules 01 to 03 spent so long on getting dip right.
  6. Choose a wavelength on purpose. Turn the short-wavelength texture up to about 20 and compare the two panels in step 5.
    HintThe short operator maps the fine fabric and loses the fold; the long operator maps the fold and loses the fabric. Both are correct computations of curvature on the same surface. If the fine fabric is fractures you want the short operator; if it is footprint you want the long one, and nothing in the data tells you which it is. That decision is yours and should be stated when the volume is handed on.

The main points

  • Curvature is the rate of change of dip. Flat beds and uniformly dipping beds both give zero. Only changes in dip produce an anomaly.
  • Bright curvature does not mean steep. Curvature peaks where the bed is flat at a crest or a trough, and passes through zero where the bed is steepest.
  • One number is not enough. A surface can curve up in one direction and down at right angles to it, so two principal curvatures are reported, together with the direction they point in.
  • k₁ and k_pos are different attributes. The principal curvatures correct for the dip the reflector already has; the most-positive and most-negative ones do not, and on dipping structure they read systematically high.
  • Operator length is an interpretation. Long-wavelength curvature maps folds, short-wavelength curvature maps fabric, and the same surface gives both.
  • Everything the dip field got wrong arrives here doubled. Curvature is a second derivative, so short-wavelength error in dip is amplified twice over.
Method, sources, and what has been simplified

The structure is a grid of depths to a single reflector, built from a small number of analytic features — two anticlines, a syncline, and a dome — on an optional regional tilt, with optional short-wavelength texture and optional noise. Depth is used rather than time so that curvature has its proper units of one over length. Working from a time-migrated volume instead requires converting the dip components with a velocity, which AASPI does with a single constant and which its documentation describes as crude; the resulting error carries into curvature magnitude but has little effect on strike or azimuth.

Curvature is computed the way a volumetric attribute computes it, not analytically. The inline and crossline dip components are found by differencing the depth grid, smoothed along the operator length, and then differenced again to give the three second derivatives. From those, with p and q the dip components:

k_mean = [(1+q²)r − 2pqs + (1+p²)t] / 2(1+p²+q²)^{3/2},   k_gauss = (rt − s²) / (1+p²+q²)², and the principal curvatures are k₁ = k_mean + √(k_mean² − k_gauss) and k₂ = k_mean − √(k_mean² − k_gauss), with k₁ ≥ k₂ by construction. The uncorrected most-positive and most-negative curvatures are the eigenvalues of the same second derivative matrix without the terms in p and q, which is exactly the dip correction the AASPI documentation says they omit. Strike comes from the eigenvector direction, ψ = ½·atan2(2s, r − t).

Apparent curvature at an arbitrary azimuth uses Euler's theorem, k(φ) = k₁cos²(φ − ψ) + k₂sin²(φ − ψ). That the two principal directions are always perpendicular is a property of smooth surfaces, not an assumption made here.

A note on the source. The AASPI curvature3d documentation gives curvedness twice, once as √(k₁² + k₂²) and once as √((k₁² + k₂²)/2); the second matches Roberts (2001) and is the one used in the next module. Its shape index likewise appears with two opposite sign conventions. Neither affects this module, which does not use either quantity, but both are worth knowing about before comparing numbers between packages.

Simplifications. This is a single surface rather than a volume, so there is no vertical derivative and none of the attributes that need one: reflector convergence, rotation about the normal, and the vertical smoothing that curvature3d applies. The operator here is a simple smoothing length rather than the fractional-derivative operator AASPI uses, which its documentation describes as a first derivative followed by a low-pass filter; the effect demonstrated is the same one. Curvedness, shape index and the shape components have a module of their own, as do amplitude curvature and aberrancy.
