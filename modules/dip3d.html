Module 02  ·  Geometric attributes

Inline and crossline dip: one trace, two numbers

In module 01 we estimated dip along a single seismic line by finding the tilt that best aligned neighboring traces. A 3D volume allows that measurement in two directions, and one direction turns out not to be enough. This module is about what changes between a line and a volume.

The reflector orientation — these two numbers drive every step

How much later the reflector arrives on the next trace along the inline direction.

How much later it arrives on the next crossline. The same reflector, measured in the other direction.

4Every trace gets its own pair

The two numbers belong to a location, not to the volume

Steps 2 and 3 produced two numbers at one trace. That pair describes the reflector there. Move to the next trace and the measurement is repeated, and repeated again at every sample down each trace. A dip volume is the result of doing this everywhere, so it holds two numbers for every sample in the input.

With a planar reflector every trace returns the same pair, so the maps below come out a single flat color. Curve the reflector and the pair starts changing from trace to trace, which is what it does in real data and the reason the measurement has to be repeated rather than assumed.

Both maps look down on the volume: inline runs left to right, crossline runs up, and every pixel is one trace coloured by the estimate made at that trace. The outer two traces on each side are left out, because the analysis patch does not fit inside the volume there — real dip volumes have the same problem at survey edges. The azimuth scale is a color wheel that returns to where it started, because 359° and 1° are nearly the same direction and a scale with different colours at its ends would draw a boundary that is not there.

5Putting the two components together

A time slice shows both at once

Steps 1 to 4 all cut the volume vertically. A time slice cuts it horizontally: pick one travel time and read the amplitude of every trace at that one time. The result is a map with a seismic value at every bin, and no time axis at all.

The diagram below shows why that map is useful. A horizontal cut through a dipping reflector meets it along a straight line. On one side of that line the slice sits above the reflector, on the other side below it, and along the line itself it is passing straight through the peak. So the reflector prints itself onto the map as a band, and the band runs along strike.

Switch the dome on and the same argument gives a different shape. A horizontal cut through a domed reflector meets it along a closed ring rather than a straight line, and that is exactly the ring you see on the slice. The diagram redraws itself to match, so you can check that the picture on the left and the sketch above it are telling the same story — the band and the ring are the same intersection, drawn on two different reflector shapes.

Magnitude is not the sum of the two components and it is not the larger of them. It is √((∂t/∂x)² + (∂t/∂y)²), and the components must be in the same units first. Inline dip is quoted per trace and crossline dip per line, so if the bins are not square those two are not comparable quantities.

?Why two components and not one number

What the dip vector buys you downstream

Module 01 found dip on a single line. Real surveys are volumes, and the moment you leave one line the question changes: a reflector does not have a dip, it has a direction it goes down and a steepness in that direction. Two numbers per sample, not one.

That extra number is not bookkeeping. Here is what it buys.

What needs the full vector

  • Steering an analysis window in 3D. A window aligned along the inline dip but not the crossline dip is still cutting across the bed, and the attribute computed in it is still measuring structure instead of discontinuity. Module 03 shows what that costs. You need both components or you have steered in one direction and not the other.
  • Curvature, and everything above it. The principal curvatures of module 05 are derivatives of both dip components. With only one you cannot form them at all — there is no k₁ and k₂ from a single number, and no shape index, and no aberrancy.
  • Structure-oriented filtering smooths along the bedding plane, which is a plane in three dimensions. One component defines a line, not a plane.
  • Fault and fracture work. Dip azimuth is what turns a dip field into a structural grain map. Lineaments that are invisible on amplitude often stand out cleanly on azimuth, because a fault changes the direction the beds go down even where it barely changes how steeply.

And a warning that comes with it

Dip azimuth is cyclic. An azimuth of 359° and one of 1° are two degrees apart, not three hundred and fifty-eight, and a color scale that does not wrap will draw a hard boundary across perfectly continuous structure — an artifact that looks exactly like a lineament. Step 4 uses a cyclic scale for this reason, and it is worth checking what your own software does before you interpret an azimuth map.

The same trap returns twice more in this course: curvature strike wraps at 180° because it is an orientation, and aberrancy azimuth wraps at 360° because it is a direction. Three attributes, three different wrapping rules, and each one draws a false edge if you get it wrong.

The practical summary: apparent dip belongs to the section you happened to shoot, and true dip belongs to the rock. Every attribute after this one is computed in the rock's frame, which is why this module exists before any of them.

Exercises

  1. Make a dipping bed look flat. Set inline dip to 0 and crossline dip to 6, then read the inline section in step 1.
    HintThe inline section shows a horizontal reflector while the bed is dipping about 21 degrees. Nothing in that section is wrong or noisy; it is cut along strike, and along strike there is no time change to see. An interpreter working a single line through this volume would report zero dip, describe the section correctly, and describe the geology not at all.
  2. Watch the pair change across the volume. Press the Flat preset, then switch the dome on in step 4 and compare the two maps before and after.
    HintWith a plane both maps are a single flat color. Every trace returns the same pair, so one measurement would have described the whole volume. With the dome on a flat regional, magnitude climbs from zero at the crest to about 0.39 ms/m at the edge, and azimuth runs right round the color wheel, because the reflector falls away in every direction. Now put the crossline dip back to 4 and switch the dome on again: magnitude runs 0.00 to 0.52 ms/m, the crest shifts off center toward the south, and the azimuth pattern leans that way rather than being symmetric — the dome and the regional dip are simply added, and the crest is wherever the two cancel. Real data behaves like these two cases rather than like the plane, which is why the estimate is repeated at every sample instead of measured once.
  3. Find the two azimuths that matter. With inline 2 and crossline 4, use step 6 to find the azimuth where the section is steepest and the azimuth where it is flat.
    HintSteepest at about 63 degrees from inline, flat at about 153, and the two are exactly 90 degrees apart. The steepest direction is the dip azimuth and equals the arctangent of the two components; the flat direction is strike. Every other section shows something in between, following a cosine. Apparent dip is therefore not a property of the reflector but of the reflector and the section together.
  4. Break the azimuth with rectangular bins. In step 5 set the inline bin to 12.5 m and leave the crossline bin at 25 m.
    HintNot one pixel of the sections or the time slice changes, because the volume is stored by bin number and the reflector still crosses the same number of traces. The computed azimuth moves from 63 degrees to 45, a swing of 18 degrees, because a step of one trace is now half the distance a step of one line is. Anything combining the two components has to divide by the bin spacing first. The error is invisible on square-bin surveys, which is how it survives into production code.
  5. Compare a coarse grid with a coarse fan. In step 7, note the number of combinations at a 1 ms step, then set the step to 0.5 ms.
    HintThe count goes from 289 to 1089, close to four times as many, because the grid is two-dimensional. In module 01, halving the step doubled the work. This is the practical reason production dip estimators do not simply search a fine grid, and why several use a gradient structure tensor instead, which solves for the orientation directly rather than trying candidates.
  6. Shrink the patch to 3 × 3. With noise at 30%, drag the patch size down from 11 × 11 and read the score alongside the error.
    HintThe score hardly moves: about 0.75 at 11 × 11 and about 0.77 at 3 × 3. The error changes by a factor of eighteen, from roughly 0.04 ms to roughly 0.70 ms per trace. Nine traces support two unknowns rather than one, and they reach only a single trace either side of the analysis point in each direction, which is barely enough to define a plane. The score is not measuring how well determined the estimate is; it is measuring how well the traces inside the window happen to agree, and a small window makes that easy.

The main points

  • Dip in a volume is a pair of numbers at a location. Inline dip and crossline dip are components of the reflector orientation at one trace, and neither on its own is "the dip".
  • The pair is measured again at every trace and every sample. A dip volume is not one measurement applied everywhere; it is millions of separate estimates.
  • Apparent dip belongs to the section, not the reflector. The same bed shows a different dip in every vertical section, following a cosine that peaks at the dip azimuth and is zero along strike.
  • A section cut along strike through a steep bed shows nothing. Zero dip on one line is not evidence of a flat reflector.
  • Combining the components requires common units. Convert per trace and per line to per meter using the bin spacing before taking a magnitude or an azimuth.
  • Time dip is still not geological dip. The conversion needs a velocity, and the volume does not contain one.
Method, assumptions, and where to read more

The volume is 25 inlines by 25 crosslines by 220 samples at 2 ms. The reflector is defined analytically as t(x, y) = t0 + px·x + py·y, plus a quadratic term when the dome is switched on, and the amplitude at each sample is a 30 Hz Ricker wavelet evaluated at the offset from that surface. The geometry is therefore exact rather than interpolated, and the true dip at any trace is the analytic derivative. The optional second and third beds are parallel copies 70 ms above and below.

The two maps in step 4 are produced by running the search at every second trace and interpolating for display, at a fixed 13 by 13 grid of candidate tilts regardless of the step-7 settings, because a full density scan on every slider movement would stutter. The decimation is a display choice, not part of the method. A production run estimates at every sample of every trace.

Azimuth is measured in degrees clockwise from the inline axis and points downdip. Real surveys are rotated relative to north, so a dip azimuth volume has to be corrected by the survey rotation before it means anything geographic, and packages differ over whether azimuth points updip or downdip and whether it is measured from north or from the inline. Check the convention before comparing two volumes.

The rotated section in step 6 is sampled at a fixed 15 m along the line, using bilinear interpolation between the four surrounding traces. That fixed spacing is a display choice. In a real volume the natural trace spacing along a section varies with azimuth — a section cut diagonally across square bins has traces √2 further apart than one cut along the inline — which is a further reason apparent dip is quoted per unit distance rather than per trace once you leave the acquisition directions.

The search is the discrete scan of Marfurt et al. (1998) extended to two components, one of the two algorithms in AASPI program dip3d. Refinement fits a parabola along each axis through the winning square. A true two-dimensional peak wants a paraboloid including a cross term; the separable form used here is what most implementations do and is accurate while the peak is not strongly skewed, which the elongated bright spot under noise shows it can be.

Simplifications carried over from module 01, plus new ones. Semblance is computed on the plain trace rather than the analytic trace. There is no Kuwahara search over shifted windows. The reflector is smooth, so the estimate is never fighting a fault inside the window. And the alternative family of estimators, which builds a gradient structure tensor and takes its principal eigenvector rather than trying candidates, is not shown here at all.
