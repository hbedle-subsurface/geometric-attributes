Module 08  ·  Geometric attributes

Aberrancy: one derivative further

Dip is the first derivative of structure. Curvature is the second. Aberrancy — also called flexure — is the third, and it is the attribute that finds a fault whose throw is too small even to bend the reflector detectably. It behaves differently from everything before it: three principal values instead of two, and vectors instead of signed numbers.

1Dip, curvature, aberrancy

The same profile differentiated once, twice and three times

You have built the first two rungs of this ladder already. Dip is how fast the reflector is changing depth. Curvature is how fast the dip is changing. Aberrancy is how fast the curvature is changing, and each step up the ladder moves the anomaly to a different place on the structure.

Watch the four panels line up. At the middle of a flexure the reflector is steepest, so dip peaks there. Curvature peaks at the two corners, one positive and one negative, where the bed turns into and out of the flexure. And aberrancy peaks between them, right at the middle, because that is where the curvature is changing fastest as it swings from one sign to the other.

That is why aberrancy is useful. A flexure gives curvature a pair of anomalies straddling it, which is awkward to pick, and gives aberrancy a single anomaly sitting exactly on it.

2Three principal aberrancies, not two

A cubic has three roots where a quadratic had two

Module 02 swept the azimuth of a section and found a single dip vector. Module 05 swept it again and found two extremes, k₁ and k₂, always ninety degrees apart. Sweep it once more for aberrancy and you get three.

The reason is the algebra. Apparent dip varies with azimuth as a first-order expression, apparent curvature as a second-order one, and apparent aberrancy as a third-order one — and a cubic can have three extrema. They are not evenly spaced, and there is no shortcut: where curvature hands you k₁ and k₂ through eigenvalues, no equivalent has been found for aberrancy, so the extrema have to be searched for. If that sounds familiar, it is exactly what module 01 did to find dip.

Up to three, and often fewer. Click around the seismic line in the header and watch the count change. Where the structure is locally one-dimensional — a single fault, a simple flank — the cubic degenerates and you get one dominant direction. All three appear only where two structural trends interfere, such as near the crossing of the two faults, which is where the analysis point starts. On this surface that is genuinely rare: fewer than one point in three hundred shows three well-separated roots. That is worth knowing before you go looking for the intermediate and minimum volumes in your own data.

3Vectors, not signed values

A magnitude that is always positive, and a direction that means something

This is the sharpest break with curvature. k₁ and k₂ are signed numbers that come with a strike: positive for an anticline, negative for a syncline, and the strike is an orientation with no front or back — 10° and 190° are the same line.

Aberrancies are vectors. The magnitude is strictly positive, and the azimuth runs the full −180° to +180° because it is a genuine direction, not an orientation. It points across the flexure, from the up-thrown side toward the down-thrown one, so an aberrancy azimuth map tells you which way a fault drops. No curvature attribute can do that.

The azimuth map uses a full 360° cyclic color scale, not the 180° one that dip azimuth and curvature strike use. That difference is the whole point: strike is an orientation and wraps at 180°, while an aberrancy azimuth is a direction and wraps at 360°. The two sides of a fault get opposite colors here, where a strike map would give them the same one.

4Why you display the total, not the maximum

A real problem with a simple fix

Having three roots raises an obvious question: which one do you look at? The natural answer — sort them by magnitude and display the largest — turns out to break in a way that is worth understanding, because it is a general hazard with any sorted attribute.

This model has two faults crossing each other, one stronger than the other. Wherever both are present, whichever is locally stronger claims the maximum root and the weaker one is demoted to the intermediate. AASPI's documentation names the consequence: a fault that is strongest in one place can be overwhelmed by a fault cutting across it somewhere else, and the maximum-aberrancy image then shows two disjoint faults, with the weaker one having moved onto the intermediate volume.

On this deliberately simple model the two faults are separated enough that the maximum map holds up fairly well — you can push it by setting both throws similar and watching the intermediate panel light up along stretches the maximum panel has dropped. The point stands regardless, and the fix costs nothing.

The fix is to stop sorting. Add the three aberrancy vectors together and display the sum: total aberrancy. Both faults stay continuous, because nothing has been thrown away.

5Where aberrancy sits in the toolkit

The end of the ladder that started with coherence

Module 05 walked a fault down in throw and watched coherence go blind while curvature kept seeing it. Aberrancy is the next rung down. Take the main fault's throw toward zero and compare all three.

A large offset breaks the reflector and coherence finds it. A smaller one only bends the reflector and curvature finds it. Smaller still and the bend itself is gentle, but its asymmetry survives — the flexure still leans one way — and that is what aberrancy responds to. This is the progression sketched in every "fault expression on seismic attributes" figure, and here it is with numbers attached.

The catch—
None of this comes free. Aberrancy needs 27 second derivatives, taken from the nine first derivatives that curvature already uses, so it runs about four times longer than curvature on its own. And being a third derivative it amplifies short-wavelength noise harder than anything else in this course — turn the noise slider up with a short operator and watch it drown.

Exercises

  1. Put the anomalies where they belong. In step 1, read where the dip, curvature and aberrancy peaks sit relative to the flexure.
    Show what to look forDip peaks in the middle of the flexure. Curvature gives two peaks, one positive and one negative, straddling it. Aberrancy gives a single peak back in the middle. Each derivative shifts the anomaly by a quarter cycle, which is why an odd derivative sits on the feature and an even one straddles it. For picking a fault, one anomaly on the fault beats two anomalies either side of it.
  2. Count the roots. In step 2, look at how many extrema the azimuth sweep has, and compare with what module 05 found for curvature.
    Show what to look forAt the starting point, near where the two faults cross, there are three: around 23°, 88° and 145°, so the gaps are 65° and 57° — nothing like curvature's rigid ninety. Now click elsewhere on the line. Over most of this surface only one root survives, because a single smoothed fault is locally one-dimensional and the cubic degenerates. Fewer than one point in three hundred here has three well-separated roots. That irregularity, and the fact that the number of roots is not even fixed, is why no eigenvalue shortcut exists and the extrema must be found by searching the azimuth — exactly as module 01 searched for dip.
  3. Break the maximum-aberrancy map. In step 4, set both faults to similar throws, then make one clearly stronger.
    Show what to look forWhere the two faults compete, each claims the maximum root over part of its length and loses it over the rest, and the pieces missing from the maximum map turn up on the intermediate one. The total map keeps both faults continuous, because a vector sum discards nothing. On this simple model the effect is mild — the faults are well separated — but it is severe on real data where many trends interfere. The general lesson is worth carrying beyond aberrancy: any attribute that sorts several components by magnitude will hand you discontinuous features wherever the ranking changes, and the discontinuity is an artifact of the sorting, not of the geology.
  4. Use the azimuth as a fault-drop map. In step 3, look at the azimuth colors on either side of a fault.
    Show what to look forThe azimuth points consistently across the flexure toward the downthrown side, so a single fault carries one color along its length rather than two. A curvature strike map cannot do this: strike is an orientation that wraps at 180°, so it cannot distinguish one side from the other. If you want to know which way a subtle fault drops, this is the attribute that answers.
  5. Find the cost. Set the operator length to 3 and put the noise up to about 16%.
    Show what to look forAberrancy is the first attribute to fall apart, before curvature and long before coherence. A third derivative multiplies the high wavenumbers three times over, so any noise in the dip field arrives cubed. Raising the operator length rescues it, at the usual cost of losing the narrowest features. In practice aberrancy demands the cleanest dip volume of anything in this course, which is another reason modules 01 to 03 spent so long on getting dip right.

The main points

  • Aberrancy is the third derivative of structure. Dip is the first, curvature the second. It is also called flexure.
  • It puts one anomaly on the feature. Curvature straddles a flexure with a positive and a negative anomaly; aberrancy sits on it with a single one.
  • There are three principal aberrancies, not two. A cubic has three roots, they are not evenly spaced, and no eigenvalue shortcut exists — they have to be searched for.
  • They are vectors, not signed values. Magnitude is strictly positive and azimuth runs the full 360°, so it tells you which way the structure drops. Curvature strike cannot.
  • Display the total, not the maximum. Sorting three roots by magnitude breaks features into disjoint segments wherever the ranking changes. The vector sum does not.
  • It is the most expensive and the most noise-sensitive attribute here. Around four times the cost of curvature, and a third derivative amplifies noise harder than a second.
Method, sources, and what has been simplified

The model is one horizon carrying a broad regional fold, cut by two faults that cross each other at an angle, each with its own throw. Each fault is smeared over a flexure width rather than being a sharp step, because a fault below seismic resolution is imaged as a monoclinal flexure and that is precisely the target aberrancy is aimed at.

What is computed here. Apparent aberrancy along an azimuth φ is the third directional derivative of the surface, a(φ) = z_xxx c³ + 3 z_xxy c²s + 3 z_xyy cs² + z_yyy s³ with c = cos φ and s = sin φ. That is a cubic form, and the three principal aberrancies are its three extrema in azimuth, found here by sampling φ and refining — a search, because as the AASPI documentation notes, no eigenvalue formulation has been found for aberrancy the way it has for curvature. Each root is stored as a vector: magnitude |a| and azimuth φ. Total aberrancy is the vector sum of the three, which is what AASPI outputs by default as ab_tot_mag and ab_tot_azim; the individual roots are available there as the maximum, intermediate and minimum aberrancy magnitude and azimuth.

Where this departs from AASPI. This is a single horizon, so the derivatives are taken in map view rather than volumetrically, and the dip normalization that a full treatment carries is omitted. AASPI computes the 27 second derivatives volumetrically by differentiating the nine first derivatives already used for curvature, reflector convergence and reflector rotation, which is why aberrancy costs roughly four times what curvature does. Following Di and Gao (2016), the coordinate system is rotated to align with local reflector dip and azimuth before the extrema are sought, which is what made the search tractable; a fully volumetric version of their horizon-based method would require rotating a large data window about every voxel, so AASPI computes the derivatives in the original unrotated frame first and rotates afterward. The numbers in this module are therefore correct in their behavior and relative magnitudes rather than directly comparable with a production volume.

The step 5 comparison uses the same coherence and curvature machinery as modules 04 and 05, so the three curves are computed on identical data with identical operator lengths and are fair to compare with one another.
