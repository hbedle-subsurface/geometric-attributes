Module 01  ·  Geometric attributes

Dip: the direction everything else is measured along

Almost no rock layer anywhere is perfectly flat and horizontal. Beds tilt, fold, thin and break, and a seismic reflector inherits all of it. So before you can measure anything about a reflector, something has to work out which way it is heading. Three steps: what dip is, finding it yourself, then watching the computer find it.

Why this one number comes before everything else

Every geometric attribute is measured along the reflector, not straight across the screen. That is only possible if something already knows which way the reflector is going — and since almost nothing is flat, that direction is different at every single sample in the volume.

So the first thing computed in any attribute workflow is a dip volume: the bed dip at every sample, estimated without a picked horizon. Everything below is then built on top of it.

What is standing on it

  • Coherence compares neighbouring traces along the bed. Compare them horizontally instead and a perfectly good dipping reflector looks broken — you end up imaging the bedding rather than the faults.
  • Curvature is the rate at which the dip changes from place to place. It is computed directly from the dip field, so it can never be better than the dip was.
  • Aberrancy goes one derivative further still, which means it inherits the dip errors and then amplifies them.
  • Structure-oriented filtering smooths along the bed. Give it the wrong direction and it will smooth straight across your reflectors, cheerfully and invisibly.

Twenty-five traces now, and the dip is hidden. The crimson line is your guess at which way the bed is going. Tilt it until it lies along a reflector rather than cutting across it — that is the same judgement you make by eye on a real section, just made explicit.

The best score you can reach is almost never 1.0, and on real seismic it is nowhere near. Traces differ for all sorts of reasons that have nothing to do with dip — noise, thinning beds, changing rock. You are not looking for a perfect match. You are looking for the best one available.

Step 3  ·  Let the computer do it

Same data, same job, every tilt tried in turn

The computer cannot see the line lying along the bed, but it can do the other half of what you just did: read the traces along a tilted line, check whether they stack up, and score it. So it tries every tilt across a range, one after another, and keeps the best. That is the entire algorithm — it is correcting for the dip of the bed by finding the tilt that lines the traces up.

The sliders deliberately run past sensible values in both directions — sensible settings teach you less than broken ones. The two switches on the right put something in the data worth breaking.

Where it looks

How far either side of flat it is allowed to tilt the line. If the real dip lies outside this, it can never find it — and it will not tell you.

How finely it works through that range. Bigger steps mean fewer tilts to try and a faster run, but a coarser answer.

After picking the best tilt, fit a curve through it and its two neighbours so the answer can land between two tries instead of on one.

How much data it judges on

How many traces it compares at once. Three is barely any evidence; twenty-one reaches 250 m either side of the point it is meant to be describing.

How much of each trace it compares. A useful rule is roughly one wavelength — about ±17 ms at 30 Hz. Push it to ±100 ms and the window spans three reflectors that may have nothing to do with each other.

Try this — and what to watch for

  1. Look in the wrong place. Press "tell me the answer" so you can see the target, then drag the search range down until the real dip falls outside it. The answer sticks at the edge of the range and stops moving. The bars are still climbing when the search runs out of tilts to try. Nothing warns you — the number that comes out looks just as respectable as a correct one. This is the commonest way a dip volume goes wrong.
  2. Take bigger steps. Set the step size to 2 ms/trace, then turn "fine-tune the winner" off and on a few times. With it off, the answer can only ever be one of the nine tilts that were tried, so it lands in chunks and is typically about four times further from the truth. With it on, fitting a curve through the winner and its two neighbours puts the answer back between the tries almost for free. That is how real software affords a coarse search. Push the step to 4 ms and the trick starts to fail, because a parabola needs the peak to still be sampled — with only five tilts across the whole range there is no peak left to fit.
  3. Reach across a fault. Switch the fault on and leave everything else alone — nine traces still lands clear of it. Now drag "traces used" up to 21. The score falls off a cliff — from about 0.98 to about 0.51 — the moment the tilted box reaches across the break, and the winning tilt becomes a compromise between two beds that are not the same bed. Notice the fault is one trace wide but the damage extends four traces either side of it, because that is how far the window reaches. This is why coherence computed on a smeared dip field gives fat, soft faults. Real software fights it by trying several windows offset around the point and keeping the most confident one, rather than always centring the window on the sample.
  4. Make the window too tall. Switch on "bed dip changes with depth", then drag the window height from ±30 ms up to ±100 ms and watch the box grow down into the lower beds. Nothing happens until about ±80 ms, then the score drops from 0.98 to around 0.70 and the answer slides toward the average of two things that have nothing to do with each other. Switch the depth change off and repeat: the same window heights now cost you nothing at all. The seismic did not get harder — your instruction about how much of it to trust at once got worse. That is why window height is really a question about how far you can assume the geology stays the same, which is why it is usually quoted as a fraction of a wavelength.
  5. Use fewer traces, and watch the score go the wrong way. Set noise to about 30%, then drag "traces used" from 9 down to 3. Read the score and the error together. The score goes up — from around 0.68 to around 0.85 — while the answer gets roughly four times worse. Three traces have less to disagree about, so they match more easily and prove much less. This is the most useful thing on this page: a high score is not evidence of a good answer, and any confidence volume has to be read next to the window that produced it.
  6. Add noise until it fails. Push noise up in steps — 20%, 40%, 60% — pressing "new hidden dip" a few times at each level. The answer degrades gently at first: barely wrong at 20%, a few tenths of a millisecond out at 40%. Then past 50% the bars stop having one clean peak and the winner occasionally lands on the wrong bump entirely. Watch the score though — it starts falling long before the answer does, from 0.99 to below 0.6. That gap is why attribute software writes a confidence volume alongside the dip: it warns you well before the number beside it goes obviously wrong.

If you remember nothing else

  • Nothing is flat. Dip is not a special case for structured areas; it is the normal condition of every reflector in every survey.
  • Dip is a time shift per trace. Turning it into an angle needs a trace spacing and a velocity, and those come from you, not from the seismic.
  • It is found by trying, not by measuring. Tilt a line every way you are allowed to, score how well the traces line up along it, keep the best. That is the whole algorithm.
  • A low score is a warning; a high score is not a guarantee. Fewer traces and shorter windows both raise the score while making the answer worse, because there is less evidence in the window to disagree.
  • The window has to be small enough that the geology inside it is one thing. Too wide and it crosses a fault; too tall and it crosses into beds going the other way.
  • Everything downstream inherits this. Coherence steers by it, curvature is computed from it, aberrancy differentiates it again. Fix the dip before blaming the attribute.
Method, assumptions, and where to read more

Twenty-five traces sampled every 2 ms, built by convolving a set of reflection coefficients with a 30 Hz Ricker wavelet and shifting each trace according to the dip. The hidden dip comes from a seeded random number, so "new hidden dip" gives a genuinely different case each time while a shared link always reproduces the same one. The optional fault is a vertical step in arrival time applied to every trace to the right of trace 17; the optional depth change reverses the dip of every bed below about 290 ms.

The score is semblance: the energy of the summed traces divided by the summed energy of the individual traces, over the analysis window. It is 1 when every trace in the window is identical and falls toward 1/N when they are unrelated. Marfurt et al. (1998) introduced it as a coherence measure, and here it is also the thing the dip search maximises — which is not a coincidence. Coherence and dip are the same calculation asked two different questions.

The search is the discrete dip scan of Marfurt et al. (1998), offered today as one of the two algorithms in AASPI program dip3d. Candidate tilts are spaced evenly across the range, each trace is read along the tilted line with linear interpolation between samples, and the winner is refined by fitting a parabola through it and its two neighbours.

Simplified deliberately, with each simplification getting its own module later: this is 2D, so there is a single dip component rather than the inline and crossline pair, and no dip azimuth. The search range is quoted in milliseconds per trace, whereas real software asks in degrees and needs a conversion velocity to get there. Semblance is computed on the plain trace rather than the analytic trace. Dip is estimated at one point rather than at every sample in a volume. And there is no Kuwahara search over shifted windows, which is what keeps a real dip estimate from smearing across a fault.
