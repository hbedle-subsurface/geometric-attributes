Dip: the direction everything else is measured along

Three steps, and nothing to know in advance. First, what dip actually is. Then, finding it yourself on nine traces by sliding them until they line up. Then, handing that job to the computer and watching it do exactly what you just did, only faster and thousands of times over.

Why start here

Coherence, curvature and aberrancy are all measured along the reflector. Before any of them can run, something has to work out which way the reflector is going — at every sample, without a picked horizon, including inside noise.

That estimate is called volumetric dip, and it is the first thing produced in any geometric attribute workflow. Get it wrong and every attribute computed afterwards is wrong in the same way, quietly.

The whole idea in one line

Dip is how much later a reflector arrives on the next trace along.

That is it. A time shift per trace. Everything in this module is about measuring that one number, and everything in the modules after it is about what you can do once you have it.

Now break it

These are the settings real attribute software asks you for, and they are usually left at whatever the program suggested. Change one at a time and watch the answer move.

Where it looks

How far either side of flat it is allowed to look. If the real dip lies outside this, it can never find it — and it will not tell you.

How finely it works through that range. Bigger steps mean fewer guesses and a faster run, but a coarser answer.

What it compares

How many traces it scores at once. More traces give a steadier answer, but a wider window smears across anything that interrupts the reflector.

How much of each trace it scores. Taller is steadier, but it mixes together reflectors that may have nothing to do with each other.

The data, and the display

Real traces are never identical. Add some and watch both the score and the accuracy fall away.

After picking the best guess, fit a curve through it and its two neighbours so the answer can land between two guesses instead of on one.

Try this — and what to watch for

  1. Look in the wrong place. Press "tell me the answer", then drag the search range down until the real dip falls outside it. The answer sticks at the edge of the range and stops moving. The bars are still climbing when the search runs out of guesses. Nothing warns you — the number that comes out looks just as respectable as a correct one. This is the commonest way a dip volume goes wrong.
  2. Take bigger steps. Set the step size to 2 ms/trace, then turn "fine-tune the winner" off and on a few times. With it off, the answer can only ever be one of the nine values that were tried, so it lands in chunks and is typically about four times further from the truth. With it on, fitting a curve through the winner and its two neighbours puts the answer back between the guesses almost for free. That is how real software affords a coarse search. Push the step to 4 ms and the trick starts to fail, because a parabola needs the peak to still be sampled — with only five guesses across the whole range, there is no peak left to fit.
  3. Use fewer traces, and watch the score go the wrong way. Set noise to about 30%, then drag "traces used" from 9 down to 3. Read the score and the error together. The score goes up — from around 0.68 to around 0.85 — while the answer gets roughly four times worse. Three traces have less to disagree about, so they match more easily and prove much less. Shrinking the window height does the same thing. This is the single most useful thing on this page: a high score is not evidence of a good answer, and any confidence volume has to be read next to what produced it.
  4. Add noise until it fails. Push noise up in steps — 20%, 40%, 60% — pressing "new hidden dip" a few times at each level. The answer degrades gently at first: barely wrong at 20%, a few tenths of a millisecond out at 40%. Then somewhere past 50% the bars stop having one clean peak and the winner occasionally lands on the wrong bump entirely, several milliseconds away. Watch the score though — it starts falling long before the answer does, from 0.99 to below 0.6. That gap is the whole reason attribute software writes a confidence volume alongside the dip: it is warning you well before the number it sits next to goes obviously wrong.

If you remember nothing else

  • Dip is a time shift per trace. Turning it into an angle needs a trace spacing and a velocity, and those come from you, not from the seismic.
  • It is found by guessing, not by measuring. Try every candidate, score each one, keep the best. That is the entire algorithm.
  • A low score is a warning; a high score is not a guarantee. Fewer traces and shorter windows both raise the score while making the answer worse, because there is less evidence in the window to disagree.
  • Every setting is a trade somebody has to make. Range, step size, traces and window height all change the answer, and the defaults are somebody else's guess about your data.
Method, assumptions, and where to read more

Nine traces sampled every 2 ms, built by convolving three reflection coefficients with a 30 Hz Ricker wavelet and shifting each trace by the dip. The hidden dip in steps 2 and 3 comes from a seeded random number, so "new hidden dip" gives a genuinely different case each time while a shared link always reproduces the same one.

The score is semblance: the energy of the summed traces divided by the summed energy of the individual traces, over the analysis window. It is 1 when every trace in the window is identical and falls toward 1/9 when they are unrelated. Marfurt et al. (1998) introduced it as a coherence measure, and here it is also the thing the dip search maximises — which is not a coincidence. Coherence and dip are the same calculation asked two different questions.

The search is the discrete dip scan of Marfurt et al. (1998), offered today as one of the two algorithms in AASPI program dip3d. Candidates are spaced evenly across the range, each trace is read at a shift proportional to its distance from the centre with linear interpolation between samples, and the winner is refined by fitting a parabola through it and its two neighbours.

Simplified deliberately, with each simplification getting its own module later: this is 2D, so there is a single dip component rather than the inline and crossline pair, and no dip azimuth. The search range is quoted in milliseconds per trace, whereas real software asks in degrees and needs a conversion velocity to get there. Semblance is computed on the plain trace rather than the analytic trace. And there is no Kuwahara search over shifted windows, which is what keeps a real dip estimate from smearing across a fault.
